# LOATHING IN THE WOES OF RUTHLESSNESS
## The Chosen One

### Skyler Blue Spillers

*A novel built from real textures, fictionalized events, and the uncertainty between them*

---

## Author's Note

This is a novel.

Some of its textures come from ordinary life: Arkansas jobs, fatherhood, school, volunteering, software projects, churches, relationships, late-night code, chess boards, phone screens, and the strange way a person's public identity can become larger than the person living inside it.

The criminal organizations, covert operations, conspiratorial structures, antagonists, and many events in this book are fictionalized or composite creations. Where the narrator believes something happened, the book does not automatically ask the reader to believe the narrator's explanation for why it happened.

That distinction is the point.

A screenshot can prove that a message existed without proving who sent it.

A coincidence can be frightening without being evidence.

A real act of manipulation can occur inside a life that also contains misunderstanding, exhaustion, rumor, algorithms, ordinary cruelty, ordinary mistakes, and chance.

This book is about what happens when all of those things begin to look the same.

It is also about three daughters.

Everything else comes after that.

# PROLOGUE
## The Archive He Meant for Three Girls

At 2:14 in the morning, the house finally stopped asking anything from him.

No cartoons muttering from the television. No dryer kicking itself off balance. No little feet crossing the hallway. No phone call he had to decide whether to answer. No manager asking where somebody was. No professor announcing that the assignment portal closed at 11:59 p.m. Eastern as though Eastern Time were a moral principle.

Only the laptop.

Its fan breathed warm air against Skyler's wrist.

The GitHub page on the screen showed another repository he had no business starting.

He already had too many.

That was one of the things people could say about him and be completely right.

He built before he finished. He dreamed in folder structures. He could turn one idea into twelve projects before breakfast and then spend three days trying to remember which one contained the code that actually worked.

On the desk beside him sat a black knight from a cheap plastic chess set. Luna had carried it around earlier because she liked the horse. Summer had drawn a blue rain cloud across the back of an envelope he needed for something important. Alexis had left half of a cracker pressed into the groove beside the laptop trackpad.

Skyler picked the cracker out with a fingernail.

"Enterprise infrastructure," he whispered.

He ate it.

The repo name field blinked.

He typed:

`hope-ai`

Deleted the hyphen.

Typed:

`HopeAI`

He stared at it.

The word *hope* had bothered him for years because people used it when they had nothing practical to offer.

Hope you'll be okay.

Hope things work out.

Hope the judge sees it.

Hope she comes back.

Hope the job calls.

Hope the bill clears.

Hope the kids understand someday.

He had started to hate the word until the shelter gave it back to him.

At Hope Campus, nobody had time for decorative hope. The coffee had to be made. The tables had to be wiped. Somebody needed socks. Somebody needed to know where to go next. Somebody needed a phone charger. Somebody needed a person to look at him while he spoke instead of looking at the clipboard.

Hope there had verbs attached to it.

Skyler liked verbs.

He clicked **Create repository**.

For a moment there was only the new blank page.

No users. No investors. No magical artificial intelligence. No giant company. No destiny.

Just a README waiting for a first sentence.

He typed:

> Build technology that helps people when systems become hard to navigate.

He read it twice.

Too broad.

Too idealistic.

Too him.

He left it.

Down the hall, one of the girls coughed in her sleep.

Skyler turned toward the sound automatically.

Nothing followed.

He looked back at the screen.

There were nights when he imagined the repositories as buildings in a city his daughters might someday visit after he was gone. Some would be empty. Some would have bad wiring. Some would be embarrassing little structures held together with copied code and ambition. A few, he hoped, would still open when they tried the door.

Maybe they would find jokes.

Maybe chess puzzles.

Maybe recordings.

Maybe the old versions of SKYCOIN4444 before he learned to describe it without making it sound like he had already built the future.

Maybe a file that said what fathers rarely say correctly while they are still alive and busy and angry and distracted:

I thought about you when you weren't here.

I was trying to make something last.

I did not always know what I was doing.

I loved you anyway.

Skyler opened a text file and named it:

`FOR_THE_GIRLS_SOMEDAY.md`

Then he stopped.

The cursor pulsed on the empty page.

He could build authentication systems. He could spend six hours arguing with TypeScript. He could learn the opening theory of a chess line he would never play over the board. He could practice Mandarin tones until the same syllable sounded like four different versions of reality.

But the sentence in front of him felt harder than all of it.

He finally typed:

> If you ever find this, start with this: none of the software mattered more than you did.

He saved the file.

The clock changed to 2:17.

His phone, face down beside the keyboard, vibrated once.

Skyler ignored it.

It vibrated again.

He turned it over.

The lock screen held no message.

Only an advertisement.

A dark-blue car sat beneath a gray sky.

The text under it read:

**YOU CAN'T BUILD YOUR WAY OUT OF EVERYTHING.**

Skyler stared at the sentence.

Then he laughed.

Not because it was funny.

Because targeted advertising had become so invasive that sometimes all a person could do was laugh at the accuracy.

He locked the phone and went back to the README.

Years later, when people asked where the story began, he would remember that advertisement.

He would be wrong.

The story had begun long before the phone learned how to frighten him.

It began when his life was still ordinary enough to lose.

# PART I — BEFORE THE GLITCH

## Chapter One
### Hope

The first thing Skyler learned at the shelter was that homelessness had a smell, and the smell was not what people thought.

It was coffee.

Burnt coffee, mostly.

Coffee that had sat on a warmer too long because nobody wanted to throw it away while somebody might still want a cup. Coffee poured into paper cups by hands that shook. Coffee carried into corners where people charged old phones and tried to call numbers they had already called yesterday.

There was laundry detergent too. Wet coats. Industrial cleaner. Cigarettes clinging to fabric. Rain when the weather turned. But coffee dominated everything because coffee was cheap, hot, legal, and one of the few things a person could offer another person without first asking him to prove he deserved it.

Skyler liked that.

He was twenty-something and still learning how many versions of adulthood could exist inside the same week.

At the nursery, adulthood meant lifting what needed lifting and knowing the difference between a plant that looked dead and one that actually was.

At school, adulthood meant discussion boards, deadlines, passwords, citations, and pretending he had read the rubric before beginning the assignment.

At church, it meant sitting still long enough to hear a message without turning every sentence into a private instruction.

At home, it meant three girls who did not care what any adult thought Skyler Blue Spillers was supposed to become.

At Hope Campus, adulthood meant noticing who had not eaten.

"You know where the cups are?" a volunteer asked him on one of his first mornings.

Skyler looked around the room as though the cups might be testing him.

"Probably where cups usually are."

"That's confidence. I asked for knowledge."

He smiled. "No, ma'am."

"Cabinet. Bottom left. Don't use the good ones unless we're out of the bad ones."

"There are good paper cups?"

"Everything has a class system if you give people enough time."

He remembered that sentence.

He remembered people more easily when they were funny.

The shelter was full of people the rest of the world had reduced to categories: homeless, addicted, unemployed, mentally ill, veteran, offender, runaway, difficult, noncompliant, unfortunate.

In person, the categories fell apart almost immediately.

A man everybody assumed could not use a computer once corrected Skyler on a phone setting.

A woman with everything she owned stuffed into two plastic bags asked him whether he had remembered to eat lunch.

A quiet man who rarely looked anyone in the eye played chess like the board had personally insulted him.

"You play?" the man asked one afternoon.

"A little."

"Everybody who says 'a little' either doesn't know how the horse moves or wants you to underestimate them."

"Knight."

"See? Already annoying. Sit down."

The set was missing one black pawn, so they used a sugar packet.

Skyler took the center too aggressively.

The man punished him for it.

"You do that in life too?" he asked.

"Do what?"

"Move every piece because you can."

Skyler looked at the board.

"Probably."

"That's exhausting."

"You don't even know me."

"Don't need to. Your queen's on vacation and your king's naked."

Skyler laughed so hard somebody at the next table looked over.

He lost eleven moves later.

The man reset the pieces.

"Again?"

Skyler checked the time.

He had class work due. A shift later. Messages waiting. Three daughters who could turn an hour into an emergency without changing clothes.

"One more."

It became three.

That afternoon, driving away, he thought about the shelter in systems language because that was how his mind worked when he cared about something.

The problem was not simply that people needed help.

It was that help had interfaces.

Forms.

Office hours.

Eligibility rules.

Phone trees.

Passwords.

Transportation.

Documentation.

Waiting lists.

Different agencies that did not always talk to one another.

A person could be surrounded by services and still fail to reach one.

Skyler thought: *Somebody should build something that helps people navigate this.*

Then he caught himself.

He had a bad habit of meeting a human problem and immediately trying to turn it into software.

Sometimes people did not need software.

Sometimes they needed the cup from the bottom-left cabinet.

He drove home through Arkansas rain that made every traffic light look doubled on the windshield.

His phone sat in the passenger seat plugged into a cable that only worked if it was bent upward near the connector.

A school notification appeared.

**Assignment due Sunday.**

A work message appeared beneath it.

**Can you cover tomorrow?**

Then a photo from the girls' mother: three children crowded into one frame, two looking at the camera, one apparently attempting to leave reality through the side of the picture.

Skyler pulled into a gas station before answering.

He enlarged the photo.

Luna was trying to look serious and failing.

Summer's hair had committed to several directions at once.

Alexis held something red in her fist.

His entire chest changed temperature.

There were men who talked about legacy as if it were a building with their name across the front.

Skyler understood it differently.

Legacy was three people who would someday decide what kind of man their father had been.

No marketing campaign could control that forever.

No title could substitute for it.

No amount of code could compile into a childhood he had missed.

He typed:

**Tell them Daddy loves them. I'll call tonight.**

Then he deleted *Daddy* and changed it to *I* because suddenly it sounded too formal.

**Tell them I love them. I'll call tonight.**

He sent it.

A truck hissed past on wet pavement.

Skyler sat with the phone in his hand and the rain drumming the roof.

He did not know then how many times, over the next several years, he would try to prove what he loved to people who had already decided what he was.

He did not know he would build companies in drafts, lose jobs, start over, write a book, fill GitHub with more repositories than any reasonable person needed, study code and Chinese and chess as if discipline could create a wall around a life.

He did not know that one day a phone screen would show him a sentence so private-feeling that he would spend months trying to determine whether somebody had put it there for him.

That day, the phone was only a phone.

He used it to call his daughters.

Luna answered first.

"Daddy?"

"Hey, baby."

"Summer took my thing."

"Hello to you too. What thing?"

"My thing."

"That narrows it down."

In the background, Summer yelled, "It was on the couch!"

Alexis began making a sound that could have been a laugh, a protest, or an attempt to summon weather.

Skyler closed his eyes.

There it was.

The whole world.

No hidden message required.

## Chapter Two
### Three Girls

Luna believed adults knew the rules.

That was the first problem.

She would ask questions with the confidence of a customer calling technical support.

Why can't we have ice cream for breakfast?

Why does school start when people are tired?

Why do grown-ups say "maybe" when they mean no?

Why can't a horse move straight in chess?

Why does God let bad stuff happen if He can stop it?

Skyler had answers for exactly one of those questions.

"Because then it wouldn't be a knight."

Luna stared at him.

"That's not an answer."

"That's chess."

"Chess is weird."

"Correct."

They sat at a kitchen table that carried evidence of every life being lived around it: a cup with dried milk at the bottom, crayons with their paper peeled off, an unopened bill, a laptop charger, two hair ties, a small church handout, and the chess board Skyler had bought because he had convinced himself fatherhood would become easier if he could turn it into activities.

Summer stood on a chair despite repeated legislation against it.

"Get down."

"I'm getting something."

"You can get it from the ground."

"It's not on the ground."

"That is not what I meant."

"Then say what you mean."

Skyler looked at her.

She was too young to have discovered cross-examination naturally.

"Who taught you that?"

Summer smiled.

That was worse.

Alexis, the youngest, had entered a phase where every object in the world fell into one of three categories: food, toy, or object to be hidden somewhere unreasonable.

Skyler found a spoon in a shoe.

A crayon in the refrigerator.

One of his earbuds in a doll's stroller.

"Where's the other one?" he asked.

Alexis pointed at Luna.

"Why are you telling on me?" Luna said.

"Because she respects justice," Skyler said.

"She can't even say justice."

"That's how pure it is."

There were moments like that when he felt almost embarrassed by happiness.

Not because anything was perfect.

Nothing was.

Money could be tight. Relationships could be tense. Work schedules moved. School waited at night. Adults carried old arguments into new rooms. Skyler could become impatient and then hate the sound of his own voice afterward.

But happiness did not require perfection.

Sometimes it required pancakes shaped approximately like circles.

Sometimes it was three girls arguing about who got the blue cup.

Sometimes it was sitting on the floor and letting Alexis stack blocks against his leg while Luna explained a cartoon plot with the seriousness of a prosecutor.

Sometimes it was Summer falling asleep in the car five minutes before arriving somewhere and becoming forty pounds heavier through a law of physics available only to sleeping children.

Skyler wanted to remember those details because he had begun to understand that memory was dishonest about scale.

Trauma made itself huge.

Ordinary love made itself small.

Years later, people could remember the courtroom and forget the cereal bowl.

They could remember the breakup and forget the bedtime story.

They could remember the accusation and forget a child asking for another glass of water because bedtime had become a negotiation strategy.

Skyler had started taking pictures not because he was good at photography but because he did not trust the future to preserve the right things.

Luna with marker on her hand.

Summer wearing rain boots when there was no rain.

Alexis asleep sideways across a couch.

A tiny shoe beside his adult work boot.

Three toothbrushes in a cup.

A drawing of a family in which Skyler's arms extended almost to the edge of the paper.

"Why are my arms so long?" he asked.

Luna shrugged.

"Because you hug us."

He took a picture of the drawing.

Then another in case the first one did not save.

That was who Skyler was before evidence became an obsession.

A man already afraid of losing things.

He simply had not learned to call the fear by that name.

That evening, after baths and arguments and somebody crying because somebody else had looked at somebody wrong, the girls finally settled.

Skyler sat in the hallway outside their rooms with his back against the wall.

His phone showed eleven notifications.

School.

Work.

Bank.

Email.

A GitHub alert.

A message from someone he loved but did not know how to talk to without turning the conversation into a referendum on the future.

He put the phone face down.

From inside the room Luna said, "Dad?"

"Yeah?"

"Are you still there?"

"Yeah."

A pause.

"Okay."

That was all she needed.

Skyler stayed in the hallway another ten minutes.

He would remember that later too.

Not because anything happened.

Because nothing did.

## Chapter Three
### The Line

The factory floor was loud enough to erase thought.

Skyler liked that about it.

When the line was moving, nobody cared what he wanted to become in five years. Nobody cared how many tabs he had open on his laptop at home. Nobody asked whether an AI platform could change education or whether blockchain had any business touching half the things people kept trying to attach it to.

Foam came down the line.

Numbers went on the sheet.

A machine needed watching.

A person needed help.

A problem had a location.

Reality had a checklist.

As a team lead, or as close to one as the shift required him to become on any given day, Skyler learned that the official process and the real process were related but not identical.

The official process lived in binders.

The real process lived in people.

A worker knew which machine made a different sound three minutes before it jammed.

Another knew how to stack material so the next station did not hate them.

Someone else could tell by smell when something was running hotter than it should.

The best employees carried undocumented knowledge in their bodies.

Skyler noticed systems because systems were easier for him than moods.

He could see bottlenecks.

He could see when people were solving the same problem twice.

He could see the absurdity of a supervisor asking why production had slowed while standing in front of the exact thing blocking production.

He was not always tactful about seeing it.

"You know your face says things before your mouth does," a coworker told him.

"That's efficient."

"That's why people want to hit you."

"Also efficient."

The coworker laughed.

Skyler worked hard enough that people sometimes mistook intensity for certainty.

He was not certain.

He was terrified of standing still.

Standing still gave the past time to catch him.

So he worked.

Then he studied.

Then he coded.

Then he made plans too large for the hours available.

Then he slept badly and called the exhaustion ambition.

At break, he opened his phone and checked a school portal.

A grade had posted.

Good enough.

He checked GitHub.

A repository had failed a workflow.

Not good enough.

He opened the error log while eating something from a vending machine that contained enough sodium to preserve a body historically.

A coworker sat across from him.

"You doing homework?"

"Fixing something."

"At your other job?"

"Kind of."

"You get paid?"

Skyler hesitated.

"Eventually is the business model."

"So no."

"Currently no."

"That's called a hobby."

"It's called a startup."

"That's a hobby wearing a button-up shirt."

Skyler laughed.

The repo on his phone belonged to an early branch of what would become the SKYCOIN4444 ecosystem—a name that sounded either memorable or ridiculous depending on the hour.

Probably both.

He had ideas for everything.

Wallets.

Education.

AI.

Social networking.

Charity.

Marketplaces.

Games.

Developer tools.

Infrastructure.

He wanted one ecosystem to contain the parts of digital life he thought existing companies treated as separate products.

It was too much.

That did not make the desire fake.

Years later he would learn to describe the portfolio more honestly: prototypes, experiments, foundations, active projects, historical branches, working pieces, broken pieces, things worth keeping, things worth deleting.

At the time he had only one category.

Future.

The line restarted.

Skyler put the phone away.

For the next four hours, he became a person with one job again.

He liked that version of himself.

The shift ended with the strange quiet that comes after removing hearing protection.

The world seemed underwater.

Outside, Arkansas heat pressed against him even though the sun was already dropping.

He sat in the car with the air conditioner running and did not move.

His hands smelled faintly industrial no matter how much he washed them.

The phone vibrated.

A message from home.

A message from school.

A message from a person he was trying not to lose.

He answered the third one first.

That decision would not ruin his life.

Life rarely respected cause and effect that neatly.

But it said something about him.

He was always trying to save the thing that felt most likely to leave.

## Chapter Four
### Dirt

Before Skyler trusted keyboards, he trusted dirt.

Dirt was honest about what it had touched.

Wet soil darkened your palms. Mulch got into your shoes. Sap stayed on your fingers longer than seemed medically reasonable. Heat announced itself before you stepped outside and then proved its argument all afternoon.

Nursery work did not care about personal branding.

A plant needed water or it did not.

A customer needed help loading something or they did not.

A pallet had weight.

A hose reached only as far as it reached.

Skyler had been around that kind of work since he was young enough for adults to give him the tasks they called "helping" because child labor sounded less charming.

He learned the names of plants unevenly.

He learned which customers wanted advice and which wanted agreement.

He learned that people could spend twenty minutes choosing a shrub and five seconds deciding how to talk to the person carrying it.

Most importantly, he learned that living things could look finished when they were not.

A plant could lose leaves and come back.

Roots could survive what the visible part did not.

Cutting something back could look violent while saving it.

Years later, when motivational language became intolerable to him, Skyler still trusted that metaphor because he had seen it literally happen.

Not everything damaged was dead.

Not everything green was healthy.

Not everything growing was supposed to be kept.

He wished human beings came with roots visible.

They did not.

One afternoon he loaded a customer's vehicle while thinking about an assignment he had not started.

The customer watched him wrestle a bag into place.

"You in school?" the man asked.

Skyler looked at his shirt, wondering if the university had somehow printed itself there.

"Yeah."

"What for?"

"Information technology. Software development."

The man looked at the plants, then at Skyler.

"So why are you here?"

It was not meant cruelly.

That almost made it worse.

Skyler gave the answer people gave when their current life and intended life had not yet agreed on a timeline.

"Working on it."

The man nodded.

"Good luck."

Skyler closed the tailgate.

He stood in the parking lot after the vehicle left and thought about that question.

Why are you here?

At the nursery.

At the factory.

At the shelter.

At school.

At church.

On GitHub at two in the morning.

In relationships already carrying too much history.

In a life that kept feeling like the draft before the real one.

Maybe that was why he built so much.

Software allowed versions.

`v1`

`v2`

`beta`

`stable`

`restore`

`final`

`final-final`

`final-final-fixed`

People did not get version numbers.

They only got consequences.

## Chapter Five
### 2:14 A.M.

By the time Skyler's GitHub profile became a map of nearly two hundred public repositories, strangers could learn a great deal about him without hacking anything.

They could see what languages he experimented with.

They could see when he committed.

They could see which ideas returned over and over: identity, AI, blockchain, education, communication, charity, security, automation.

They could see ambition in the multiplication of projects.

They could see correction in the README files where he later learned to separate prototypes from production systems.

They could see a young developer trying to turn a sprawling identity into something inspectable.

What they could not see was the room.

The room mattered.

The room contained the difference between a repository and a life.

There was usually laundry somewhere.

A drink going warm.

A chess game paused in another tab.

A Duolingo-style Mandarin exercise reminding him that `mā`, `má`, `mǎ`, and `mà` could take the same basic sound and bend meaning through tone.

Skyler loved and hated that lesson.

Tone changed meaning.

Context changed meaning.

Timing changed meaning.

A sentence typed at noon could sound different at 2:14 in the morning.

A message from a girlfriend could mean *I need space* or *I am leaving* depending on what fear was already in the room.

A supervisor saying *we need to talk* could mean scheduling or termination.

A pastor saying *some of you need to let go* could be a sermon or a coincidence sharp enough to feel personal.

A targeted advertisement could be an algorithm or a warning if the wrong sentence appeared beneath the right image.

The human brain was a meaning engine with no off switch.

Skyler's was overclocked.

That night he moved between tabs.

GitHub.

School.

A cloud console.

A chess analysis board.

A Chinese lesson.

A draft chapter of a book he had no idea how to finish.

A photo folder.

The image of Luna's drawing with the long arms.

He opened it and smiled.

Then he returned to code.

The project on screen was not one project anymore. SKYCOIN4444 had become an umbrella over too many ambitions: social tools, AI, education, blockchain experiments, marketplaces, communication, security, infrastructure.

He knew how it sounded.

He also knew the only way to make something less ridiculous was to make one part of it real.

So he fixed a failing test.

Then another.

At 2:11 a.m., the build passed.

He leaned back and stared at the green check.

Small victory.

Real victory.

He typed a commit message:

`make the claim match the code`

He liked it enough to write it on a sticky note.

Three minutes later, his phone vibrated.

That was the advertisement from the prologue.

Dark-blue car.

Gray sky.

**YOU CAN'T BUILD YOUR WAY OUT OF EVERYTHING.**

Skyler's first reaction was amusement.

His second was irritation.

His third was professional curiosity.

He tapped the small information icon.

The ad refreshed.

Now it was for a cloud service.

He went back.

Gone.

He took a screenshot from memory by opening a note and typing the sentence.

That was important later.

The screenshot did not prove the ad had existed.

The note proved only that, at 2:18 a.m., Skyler wrote down what he remembered seeing.

Years of confusion would eventually teach him to respect that difference.

At 2:19, another notification appeared.

Not an advertisement this time.

A music recommendation.

The cover art showed a cracked blue mirror.

The title was:

**THE MAN WHO BUILT TOO MANY DOORS**

Skyler stared.

He felt a pressure behind his ribs.

He had used the phrase *too many doors* earlier that night in a draft note about software architecture.

Had he typed it into a cloud service?

Probably.

Was that service connected to advertising data?

Maybe.

Had the music app used cross-platform behavioral targeting?

Possibly.

Had he seen the phrase somewhere else first and unconsciously reused it?

Also possible.

He opened the track.

Instrumental.

No lyrics.

Thirty-eight seconds of low piano and static.

He almost laughed again.

Then, at second thirty-one, four notes played.

Skyler stopped moving.

The sequence meant nothing.

That was why it bothered him.

He replayed it.

Four notes.

Again.

Four.

On the desk, the sticky note read:

**make the claim match the code**

His phone buzzed once.

A message appeared from an account with no profile image.

**Good rule. Use it on your life.**

Skyler's mouth went dry.

He did not answer.

He took a screenshot.

This time the screenshot worked.

He took another.

Then he opened the account.

Nothing.

No history.

No followers.

No visible creation date.

A blank profile with one message.

He checked the girls' rooms.

He knew that made no logical sense.

A strange message on a phone did not imply danger in a bedroom.

He checked anyway.

Luna was asleep with one arm above her head.

Summer had kicked off her blanket.

Alexis had rotated sideways.

Skyler pulled Summer's blanket back over her.

In the hallway he looked at the screenshot again.

**Good rule. Use it on your life.**

His first instinct was to solve it.

His second was to post it.

His third, the one that would matter most, was to write down exactly what he knew before deciding what it meant.

He opened a notebook.

At the top of a page he wrote four words:

**OBSERVED**

**VERIFIED**

**INTERPRETATION**

**UNKNOWN**

Under **OBSERVED**, he wrote:

- 2:14-ish — dark-blue car advertisement with sentence about building.
- 2:19 — music recommendation with title similar to phrase in private draft.
- 2:22 — anonymous account message: "Good rule. Use it on your life."
- Screenshot of message saved.

Under **VERIFIED**, he wrote:

- Screenshot verifies message displayed on my device.
- Does not verify sender identity.

Under **INTERPRETATION**, his pen stopped.

He wanted to write:

*Someone is watching me.*

Instead he wrote:

- Message may be targeted to me personally.

Under **UNKNOWN**:

- How sender saw / guessed commit-message idea.
- Whether ad and music title are related.
- Whether any event is malicious.

Skyler looked at the page.

The four columns made the fear smaller without making it disappear.

That would become important.

For the next few years, the world would repeatedly offer him a story faster than it offered him proof.

Some stories would be false.

Some would be scams.

Some would be misunderstandings.

Some would be cruel jokes.

Some would become evidence.

And once—only once would have been enough—something he had dismissed as impossible would turn out to have been deliberately engineered.

After that, disbelief would never feel safe again.

The phone buzzed.

Another message.

**NOW YOU'RE PAYING ATTENTION.**

Skyler did not touch it.

From one of the bedrooms came a sleepy voice.

"Dad?"

He turned away from the screen.

"Yeah, baby?"

"Water."

He stood.

The phone buzzed again.

He left it on the desk.

That was the first choice he made correctly.

He did not know yet how many wrong ones were coming.

# PART II — THE LIFE THAT BROKE

## Chapter Six
### 2020

The courtroom did not look important enough to change a life.

That was Skyler's first memory of it.

No marble columns. No cinematic echo. No giant seal descending from the ceiling to tell everybody that history was happening.

Just chairs.

Paper.

A clock.

People who already knew where to stand.

And Skyler, trying to understand how much of himself he was allowed to bring into a room where emotion could be interpreted as evidence.

His lawyer had given him advice that made sense and felt impossible.

Do not interrupt.

Do not react visibly.

Do not look angry.

Do not shake your head.

Answer the question asked.

Do not try to answer the question you wish had been asked.

Skyler nodded.

Then immediately asked, "But what if something they say is wrong?"

The lawyer looked at him.

"Especially then."

That sentence stayed with him.

Especially then.

A life could be changed by sentences shorter than the life they described.

Responsible.

Unstable.

Angry.

Safe.

Unsafe.

Credible.

Not credible.

Cooperative.

Difficult.

Each word looked small on paper.

None felt small when attached to a father.

The book does not need to tell the reader exactly which allegation was made in which room by which real person.

What mattered to Skyler was the experience of hearing his life translated into institutional language and realizing that translation could become more powerful than memory.

He began collecting documents.

At first this was rational.

Dates.

Messages.

Schedules.

Receipts.

Screenshots.

Then the archive grew.

He learned a dangerous lesson from a legitimate need:

If evidence could protect him once, maybe enough evidence could protect him from everything.

It could not.

But he would spend years finding the limit.

Outside the courtroom, one of his daughters asked a question he would remember long after the adult dialogue blurred.

"Is the judge a doctor?"

Skyler crouched so they were eye level.

"No."

"Then why does he decide if people are okay?"

Skyler opened his mouth.

Nothing responsible came out.

So he said, "It's a different kind of job."

She accepted this because children sometimes grant adults mercy without knowing it.

Skyler stood.

He looked down the hallway at people carrying folders full of lives.

Years later, when his phone began feeding him patterns, when strangers seemed to know things he thought were private, when jobs fell apart and relationships became impossible to separate from fear, he would keep returning to that hallway.

Not because the hallway caused everything.

Because it taught him the terror of being described by someone else.

That was the wound.

The rest of the story would learn how to press on it.
