# LOATHING IN THE WOES OF RUTHLESSNESS: THE CHOSEN ONE
## Reality Pass V2 — Grounded Memoir-Thriller Blueprint

This document replaces the old drafting assumption that the story becomes compelling by making the conspiracy larger. The new assumption is the opposite:

> **The stranger the story becomes, the more ordinary and verifiable the life underneath it must feel.**

The reader should believe the kitchen, the factory floor, the shelter, the nursery, the school portal, the GitHub commits, the chess board, the church parking lot, the custody handoff, the unpaid bill, and the three little girls **before** the reader is asked to enter the psychological-techno-thriller.

The result should read less like a fictional hacker mythology and more like a literary memoir colliding with a paranoid techno-thriller.

---

# 1. Core correction

The previous version opens with a thirty-six-year-old fictional founder, fictional technology, a mysterious phone, a secret network, a philanthropist rumor, and a ransom thread. That creates distance before the reader has any reason to care.

V2 moves the extraordinary material later.

The protagonist is closer to the public biographical version of the author:

- **Name:** Skyler Blue Spillers, usually called Skyler or Skyler Blue.
- **Age at present-day frame:** twenty-six.
- **Home:** Arkansas.
- **Father:** three daughters — Luna, Summer, and Alexis.
- **Work history used as neutral texture:** nursery work; manufacturing/team-lead experience; customer service; software development; founder work.
- **Education:** information technology / software development study, including Southern New Hampshire University references where appropriate.
- **Service:** homeless-shelter/community volunteering and Make-A-Wish references may be used as positive biographical texture.
- **Interests:** chess, software, AI, cybersecurity, cloud systems, blockchain, learning Chinese, writing.
- **Builder identity:** Innovative Information Technology Resolutions, SKYCOIN4444, HopeAI, SkySchool, and SkyHope are part of the real public-facing creative/technical identity.
- **Current technical reality:** the public GitHub profile describes 194 public repositories as of September 15, 2026 and explicitly distinguishes prototypes, experiments, foundations, and production claims.

These details are useful because they are specific, imperfect, human, and difficult to fake on the page.

## Important legal/story boundary

Real employers, schools, charities, relatives, partners, police officers, judges, churches, doctors, companies, governments, and public figures must **not** be presented as participants in fictional criminal conduct unless that conduct is independently documented and suitable for publication.

When the story enters disputed experiences, psychological pressure, alleged hacking, corruption, cartel imagery, staged media, threats, or covert operations, use:

- fictional institutions;
- composite characters;
- changed identifying details;
- the narrator's perception;
- documentary uncertainty;
- explicit distinctions between **what happened**, **what Skyler believed it meant**, and **what could actually be proven**.

The point is not to weaken the story. It makes the story more believable.

---

# 2. The new story promise

Skyler is not introduced as a chosen hacker, millionaire founder, or target of a global operation.

He is introduced as a young father trying to become the kind of man his daughters will remember.

He works ordinary jobs. He volunteers. He studies. He goes to church. He codes too late. He makes mistakes. He falls in love badly. He loses relationships. He gets angry. He tries to rebuild. He becomes obsessed with building software that might outlast him. His GitHub account becomes part workshop, part diary, part evidence locker, part inheritance.

Then his normal life begins to feel edited.

A phrase from a private conversation appears in a song recommendation.

A stranger repeats wording from a message.

A job contact uses a sentence that appeared in an anonymous account.

A relationship argument seems to reappear in the feed.

A church phrase lands too precisely.

A vehicle waits too long.

A post appears at a time he does not remember making it.

Some events have ordinary explanations.

Some are later shown to have been deliberate manipulation.

Some remain unresolved.

The suspense comes from forcing the reader to experience the same problem Skyler experiences:

> **How do you remain rational when some of the impossible things are real, but not all of them are?**

# 3. Narrative structure

The novel uses four interlocking forms.

## A. Lived scenes

Close, sensory scenes written like literary fiction. The reader smells foam and machine oil on a manufacturing floor, wet soil at a nursery, burnt coffee at a shelter, cheap laundry detergent in an apartment, rain on Arkansas pavement, warm laptops, old church carpet, fast-food wrappers beside a chess board, and the plastic smell of a phone charging beside a hospital-style chair.

## B. Archive fragments

Short documentary inserts between scenes:

- Git commit messages;
- job schedules;
- school assignment due dates;
- chess notation;
- screenshots described in prose;
- unsent texts;
- journal entries;
- court-date memories without reproducing confidential records;
- evidence classifications;
- timestamps;
- repository names;
- phone notes;
- drafts to daughters;
- email subject lines;
- calendar fragments.

These should look mundane enough to feel true.

## C. Memory cuts

The present can trigger an earlier scene through an object rather than exposition:

- the smell of industrial foam -> team-lead shift;
- wet potting soil -> childhood/nursery work;
- a shelter coffee urn -> Hope Campus volunteering;
- a yellow crayon -> Luna;
- rain on glass -> Summer;
- a shell -> Alexis;
- a chess knight -> custody / sacrifice motif;
- a four-tone Mandarin exercise -> the difficulty of hearing meaning accurately.

## D. Evidence ledger

As the thriller intensifies, Skyler creates four columns:

1. **Observed** — what physically or digitally happened.
2. **Verified** — what an independent source supports.
3. **Interpretation** — what Skyler thinks it means.
4. **Unknown** — what he cannot responsibly claim.

This becomes the moral architecture of the novel.

# 4. Character correction

## Skyler

He should be talented but visibly unfinished.

He is not a flawless genius. He builds too much. He sometimes confuses ambition with progress. He can turn a problem into a crusade. He wants to be believed so badly that he sometimes explains too much. He loves hard, becomes attached fast, and hates feeling replaceable. He is capable of tenderness, arrogance, generosity, impatience, humor, fear, and stubborn work in the same day.

His central wound is not "being chosen."

It is **losing control of the story of his own life**.

His deepest fear is that his daughters will someday know him only through somebody else's description.

His deepest desire is therefore not wealth, revenge, or fame.

It is to leave something behind that says:

> I was here. I loved you. I tried. Here is what I built. Here is what I got wrong. Here is what I learned.

## The daughters

They are not plot devices and must never become generic "leverage."

Give each daughter ordinary, affectionate specificity. Do not make them symbols until after the reader has experienced them as children.

- **Luna:** questions, colors, chess-piece curiosity, wanting to understand grown-up rules.
- **Summer:** weather/rain motif, stubborn humor, sensory memories.
- **Alexis:** seashell / tiny-object motif, youngest-child physicality, sleep, voice, hands.

The names may remain in the autobiographical-fiction version because they already function as the emotional center, but publication should still consider privacy and future consent.

## Romantic partners

Use composite names and changed details. The emotional truth is more useful than identifying a real person.

The recurring relationship lesson is:

> Skyler keeps asking love to prove that he is safe. Eventually he learns that love cannot function as an evidence system.

## Antagonists

No omnipotent cabal.

The real terror comes from fragmented actors with limited power:

- a scammer;
- an opportunistic criminal intermediary;
- a troll network;
- a compromised account;
- an employer reacting to rumors;
- an algorithm optimizing engagement;
- a person who enjoys provoking him;
- one or two genuinely malicious operators;
- ordinary people making frightened decisions;
- Skyler's own exhausted pattern-recognition filling gaps.

Later, these can be organized into the fictional **Pawns Network**, but the reader should first encounter effects, not mythology.

# 5. New five-part structure

# PART I — BEFORE THE GLITCH

## Chapter 1 — Hope

Open at a homeless shelter/community-support setting, not with hacking.

Skyler volunteers while also trying to keep school, work, fatherhood, church, and a relationship together. A man at the shelter carries everything he owns in one bag. Skyler notices that a bad system turns human beings into case numbers. The seed for "HopeAI" is emotional before it is technical.

End with Skyler driving home exhausted while one of his daughters sleeps in the back seat.

## Chapter 2 — Three Girls

A fatherhood chapter. Breakfast, cartoons, hair, missing shoes, car seats, cheap food, bedtime, arguments, laughter. Make the love concrete.

The reader should understand exactly what can later be lost.

## Chapter 3 — Shift Lead

Manufacturing floor. Noise. Foam. Quality checks. Being responsible for people while still figuring out adulthood. Use the public employment period as texture without attaching later criminal events to the real employer.

Skyler learns the difference between a process on paper and what people actually do under pressure.

## Chapter 4 — Dirt Under the Fingernails

Nursery work / plant work. Arkansas heat. Wet soil. Loading. Customers. Work that existed before the software identity.

This chapter establishes that Skyler is not a character who appeared out of the internet.

## Chapter 5 — The Laptop at 2:14 A.M.

School portal open. Code editor open. Kids' items nearby. Bills. GitHub. First SKYCOIN4444 folders. Early HopeAI notes. Chess game in another tab. Mandarin lesson unfinished.

The ambition is impressive and a little frightening because the reader sees what it costs.

End with a commit message that reads like a promise to the future.

# PART II — THE LIFE THAT BROKE

## Chapter 6 — 2020

Do not begin with a claim about murder, conspiracy, or corruption.

Begin with the emotional mechanics of a legal/family crisis: waiting rooms, paperwork, a lawyer telling him how to sit, a child misunderstanding what a judge does, adults speaking about his life while he is present.

The factual status of accusations remains bounded.

The key line: **He learned that a life could be changed by sentences shorter than the life they described.**

## Chapter 7 — The Breakup

A relationship becomes a referendum on identity. Shame, anger, infidelity/jealousy themes can be fictionalized. Skyler responds by trying to replace grief with motion.

## Chapter 8 — Two Girls and a Bad Joke

Use the "two exotic dancers" material as character evidence, not bragging. He is hurt, reckless, lonely, and trying to prove he is desirable. The scene can be funny and sad simultaneously.

This becomes one of the moments he later recognizes as choosing spectacle over healing.

## Chapter 9 — Seven Jobs

Instead of claiming one mastermind caused every employment problem, show a montage of ordinary instability: attendance, stress, rumor, performance, bad fit, conflict, layoffs, access reviews, and one or two events that genuinely look manipulated.

The ambiguity is the point.

## Chapter 10 — Church

Faith, community, shame, belonging, and the danger of interpreting every sermon as personal. A pastor says something that lands too accurately. It may be coincidence. It may be gossip. It may be manipulation. Skyler cannot tell.

# PART III — THE GLITCH

## Chapter 11 — The Phone

Now introduce the existing phone anomaly sequence.

Because the reader already knows the private life, a feed reference to a daughter, old car, job phrase, or relationship argument now carries real weight.

## Chapter 12 — The Song

A song/recommendation seems to narrate his day. No magical certainty. Describe exactly what words were heard, where, by whom, and what alternate explanations exist.

## Chapter 13 — The Drop

The counterfeit-money / vehicle / warehouse thread enters through a fictionalized intermediary. Skyler does not participate. He preserves what he can and makes a bad decision by chasing one clue too far.

## Chapter 14 — The Offer

A recruiter/opportunist recognizes Skyler's technical identity and offers money, status, and belonging. The "dark web operator" line is retained as fictional thriller material.

The temptation works because the reader has seen his financial and emotional instability.

## Chapter 15 — The Mirror

Skyler discovers a dossier built mostly from ordinary public data: posts, GitHub, court-accessible material, photos, job history, relationship clues, purchases, location habits.

The terror is not magic. It is aggregation.

# PART IV — THE WAR FOR THE STORY

## Chapter 16 — GitHub Never Sleeps

His repositories become a second narrative of his life. Commit timestamps show where he was mentally: manic productivity, rebuilding, fear, legacy work, children's Easter eggs, unfinished products, honest audits.

The book uses real technical ambition without inflating every repository into a finished company.

## Chapter 17 — 64 Squares

Chess becomes the cleanest system in the novel because every piece is visible. Skyler loves that nothing is hidden under the board. He begins noticing how often he tries to force real life to behave like chess.

## Chapter 18 — Four Tones

Mandarin study becomes a metaphor: the same syllable can mean something different depending on tone. Skyler realizes the same is true of evidence. Context changes meaning.

## Chapter 19 — Everybody Has a Version

Friends, family, employers, strangers, ex-partners, online accounts, and Skyler himself each hold incompatible versions of what is happening.

The Pawns Network emerges not as a world government but as a brokerage exploiting those contradictions.

## Chapter 20 — The Truth Protocol

Observed / Verified / Interpretation / Unknown.

For the first time, Skyler deletes a theory he desperately wants to be true because the evidence does not support it.

That is a bigger victory than hacking anyone.

# PART V — THE CHOSEN ONE

## Chapter 21 — Not Chosen by God, Chosen by People

The phrase "chosen one" is redefined. He was useful because he was visible, wounded, technical, reactive, ambitious, and emotionally credible.

## Chapter 22 — The Children Are Not Evidence

He refuses to use private family material to win public sympathy.

## Chapter 23 — What I Got Wrong

A brutal chapter of self-accountability. He names mistakes without accepting false accusations. This prevents the book from becoming self-mythology.

## Chapter 24 — What They Actually Did

Only the independently supportable criminal acts are presented as confirmed inside the fictional investigation. Everything else stays labeled.

## Chapter 25 — The Choice

He can publish everything, retaliate, join the operation, disappear, or build.

He chooses to build.

## Epilogue — Easter Eggs

Years later, the daughters open an archive of repositories, letters, chess puzzles, audio messages, jokes, photographs, and small software Easter eggs.

They do not receive a father's conspiracy.

They receive a father.

The final line should not be a threat.

It should be something like:

> **The world never gave him the last word. So he stopped asking for it and left the girls something better: enough truth to make their own.**

# 6. High-impact recurring details

Use these repeatedly but naturally:

- Arkansas rain on windshield glass.
- Cheap coffee at volunteer/work locations.
- Plant soil beneath fingernails.
- Manufacturing ear protection and the sudden silence after removing it.
- A child asking a question adults cannot answer.
- GitHub commit times after midnight.
- A chess board left mid-position.
- Chinese tone practice playing from a phone.
- School assignments beside production code.
- Church carpet and fluorescent lights.
- Job badges collected in a drawer.
- A notebook page divided into four evidence columns.
- The number four as a motif, but not supernatural proof.
- The daughters' three recurring objects: chess piece / rain drawing / shell.

# 7. What to remove or reduce from the old version

Reduce:

- invented billionaire inheritance mythology near the opening;
- early ransom material;
- omniscient antagonists;
- unexplained secret technology;
- dialogue where every character speaks in thriller slogans;
- scenes where coincidences are automatically treated as proof;
- fake corporate biography that contradicts the author's recognizable life;
- making every job loss, breakup, song, church statement, and stranger part of one operation;
- repeated declarations that Skyler is uniquely important.

Keep, but earn later:

- the phone;
- the songs;
- dark-blue vehicle motif;
- "the drop";
- Ghost/Mara;
- Thorne;
- Pawns Network;
- Truth Protocol;
- four doors;
- the final refusal to become the villain he hates.

# 8. Voice standard

The prose should sound like a person remembering a life, not a trailer announcing a franchise.

Prefer:

> The factory was loud enough to erase thought. Skyler liked that about it. When the line was moving, nobody cared what a stranger had posted about him at two in the morning. Foam came down the belt. Numbers went on the sheet. Somebody needed help. He helped them. For eight hours, reality had a checklist.

Over:

> The Network watched from the shadows as the Chosen One entered the factory.

Prefer:

> He did not know whether the sermon was about him. That uncertainty was worse than knowing.

Over:

> The church had joined the operation.

Prefer:

> The screenshot proved that the message existed. It did not prove who sent it.

That sentence is the book's ethical center.

---

# 9. Source discipline

Public biography was cross-checked against the author's current public GitHub profile and public personal-site material. LinkedIn itself was not reliably accessible through automated public browsing during this pass, so **no detail should be described as coming from a LinkedIn post unless the post text is later supplied or independently retrieved**.

When a future source is added, record:

- exact source;
- date;
- what is fact;
- what is the author's interpretation;
- whether a third party is identifiable;
- whether publication would create unnecessary privacy or defamation risk.

The goal is a book that feels more real because it is more disciplined, not because it makes more accusations.
