## Insert Chapter — Mother's Kitchen

Skyler's mother did not have a theory.

That was why he stopped telling her things for a while.

He called one evening after receiving another anonymous message and began with, "Don't say it's a coincidence."

She answered, "Hello to you too."

"I'm serious."

"I know. That's the problem."

He was sitting in his car outside her house because he had not decided whether to go inside.

"Somebody sent something only I should know."

"Only you?"

"Me and a couple people."

"Then not only you."

Skyler closed his eyes.

"This is why I don't tell you."

"Because I ask a question?"

"Because you always minimize it."

His mother became quiet.

"Come inside."

"I'm fine."

"I didn't ask. Come inside."

He did.

Her kitchen looked nearly identical to itself across years. Same drawer that stuck. Same collection of cups accumulated from places nobody remembered visiting. Same refrigerator crowded with magnets and papers whose importance could not be inferred from placement.

She set food in front of him without asking.

"Eat."

"I'm not hungry."

"Then sit near food while you explain the end of civilization."

Skyler laughed despite himself.

"You think I'm ridiculous."

"I think you're my son. Those are different categories."

He showed her the message.

She read it.

"That's creepy."

The validation surprised him.

"Exactly."

"Do you know who sent it?"

"No."

"Do you know how they knew?"

"No."

"Then creepy is what we know."

Skyler stared.

"You and Lena secretly coordinate wording?"

"Who's Lena?"

"Never mind."

His mother sat across from him.

"You want me to tell you the whole thing you believe is true because you think that's how love works."

The sentence landed too accurately.

"No."

"Yes."

"I want you to believe me."

"I believe you got the message."

"Not enough."

"Then what would be enough?"

Skyler had no answer.

For someone who believed a secret society was pressuring him?

Agreement that it existed.

Then agreement about motives.

Then agreement about who participated.

Then agreement about old events.

Belief expanded to consume every unknown.

His mother watched him realize it.

"If I tell you everything is connected," she said, "and I'm wrong, I make you more scared. If I tell you nothing is happening, and I'm wrong, I make you less safe. So I can do something else."

"What?"

"Help with what we actually know."

She pointed at the food.

"We know you haven't eaten."

"Mom."

"We know you're not sleeping."

"That's not—"

"We know somebody sent a creepy message. We know you can save it. We know you can tell the right people. We know sitting in your car reading it fifty times won't identify them."

Skyler looked down.

The plate blurred slightly.

He blinked.

His mother pretended not to notice.

"You were a normal kid," she said.

He laughed wetly.

"What does that mean?"

"It means this thing you're going through isn't the only version of you."

"I know."

"Do you?"

There it was again.

The people who loved him had become mirrors he kept accusing of distortion because they refused to reflect only the crisis.

He ate.

The message remained unexplained.

The world did not end.

That night, his mother did not become an investigator.

She became his mother.

It helped more.

## Insert Chapter — Hanna's Rule

Hanna's rule was simple.

"No emergency created by an app gets to interrupt dinner unless there is an actual human emergency attached to it."

Skyler stared at her.

"That's broad."

"Good."

"Bank fraud?"

"Human emergency attached."

"Work outage?"

"Depends if people can die."

"My systems don't control hospitals."

"Then eat your pasta."

"Anonymous threat?"

Hanna paused.

"Specific credible threat requiring action? We act. Weird message designed to make you stare at your phone? It can wait twenty minutes."

"Twenty?"

"Thirty now."

"This negotiation is hostile."

"Eat."

The rule became a ritual.

Phone face down.

Meals mattered because meals were embodied time.

Taste.

Temperature.

Conversation.

A thing could become cold while fear remained indefinitely reheatable.

One evening, Skyler's phone buzzed three times during dinner.

His eyes moved toward it automatically.

Hanna saw.

"Human emergency?"

"Don't know."

"Then you don't know."

He laughed.

"That's deeply annoying."

"Thank you."

They kept eating.

Afterward, Skyler checked.

One school email.

One chess notification.

One unknown message:

**SHE MAKES YOU SOFT.**

His stomach tightened.

Hanna was washing dishes.

He could show her.

The message concerned her.

Was withholding it disrespectful?

Was showing it recruiting her into fear?

Boundaries were harder than rules because context mattered.

"I got something about you," he said.

Hanna turned off the water.

"Threat?"

"More like manipulation."

"Does it contain private information about me?"

"No."

"Do I need to see it for safety?"

"Probably not."

"Then I don't want to."

Skyler stared.

"That easy?"

"No. That deliberate."

"What if they're trying to isolate me from you?"

"Then making our whole relationship about them would be efficient."

He leaned against the counter.

"You're good at this."

"I'm good at knowing I don't want strangers setting the agenda for dinner."

The relationship did not become perfect because of one boundary.

Skyler still overexplained.

Hanna still sometimes withdrew when the story became too consuming.

He occasionally interpreted withdrawal as suspicious before catching himself.

She occasionally underestimated how frightening a real targeted message felt.

They apologized.

Then failed differently.

That was intimacy.

Not perfect attunement.

Repair.

The network's profiling system treated relationships as leverage because leverage assumed people were predictable under pressure.

Love became dangerous to the model when it included boundaries.

A person who refused to prove devotion on command could not be easily turned into a surveillance instrument.

A person who could say *I care and I don't know* broke the binary of loyal/betrayer.

Thorne's model had a field for **relationship dependency**.

It did not have a field for **secure disagreement**.

That omission eventually mattered.

## Insert Chapter — Lena's Notebook

Skyler once asked Lena why she still used paper.

"Because nobody can remotely change what I wrote."

He looked at her.

"That's paranoid."

She stared.

They both started laughing.

Her notebook was small, black, and ugly.

She numbered pages.

Dated interviews.

Marked direct quotes separately from impressions.

Used question marks aggressively.

Skyler hated the question marks.

"Why did you put one there?"

"Because source says it. Not verified."

"But Mara knows."

"Mara remembers."

"Same thing in this case."

"No."

"You make language impossible."

"Reality did that first."

Lena showed him a page from their earliest conversation.

**S believes phone displaying private-seeming material.**

Under it:

**Possibilities: public data, account compromise, targeted harassment, pattern fixation, combination.**

Skyler pointed.

"Pattern fixation."

"Yes."

"So you thought I was imagining it."

"No. I thought your interpretation could be partly shaped by attention. It was one hypothesis."

"Before you knew any of the real stuff."

"Correct."

"And after you learned there was real manipulation?"

She turned several pages.

**Confirmed targeted activity does not validate all prior interpretations. Reassess individually.**

Skyler leaned back.

"You really are unbearable."

"Occupational hazard."

He respected the continuity.

Lena had not moved from disbelief to belief.

She had moved individual claims between categories as evidence changed.

That was different.

Skyler began understanding why her skepticism felt safer than unconditional support.

Someone who believed everything could not detect a lie that flattered him.

Someone who dismissed everything could not detect a threat that embarrassed them.

Trust required a person willing to disappoint both narratives.

## Insert Chapter — The Apology List

Skyler made the apology list after Mara said, "You're going to publish everybody else's accountability and none of yours?"

He hated her for approximately ten seconds.

Then opened a document.

People he owed apologies.

Former partner.

Mother.

Friends.

Coworker.

Hanna.

Lena.

The girls, in age-appropriate ways.

Maybe himself, though that felt self-indulgent.

He began with Marcus.

"I owe you an apology."

Marcus said, "How much money?"

"Serious."

"That was serious."

"For treating you like if you didn't agree with me, you were part of the problem."

Marcus became quiet.

"Yeah. You did that."

"I know."

"It sucked."

"I know."

"You scared me sometimes."

Skyler looked down.

"I know now."

"I didn't know whether somebody was actually messing with you or whether you were falling apart or both."

"Both was closest."

Marcus nodded.

"That would've been useful to know."

They sat with it.

No immediate forgiveness ceremony.

Apology did not purchase closure.

That was another form of consent Skyler had once misunderstood.

He could offer repair.

The other person decided what to do with it.

His mother accepted hers with less drama.

"I know you were scared."

"Still doesn't make it okay."

"No."

Skyler smiled sadly.

"You could at least argue."

"Why? You're finally doing my work for me."

Hanna's apology was harder.

"I treated your boundaries like evidence you didn't care."

"Sometimes."

"And I wanted reassurance you couldn't actually give."

"Often."

"You're enjoying this."

"A little."

They smiled.

Then Hanna said, "I need you to understand something."

"Okay."

"You being targeted in some real ways doesn't mean people who set boundaries were wrong."

"I know."

"Say it like you mean it."

Skyler breathed.

"Danger doesn't cancel consent."

"Good."

There was that word again.

The girls received simpler apologies.

Sorry I missed that.

Sorry I was on the phone.

Sorry adults made things confusing.

Sorry I promised before I knew.

Sorry I got angry.

Children did not need the dossier.

They needed repair in the scale they lived.

## Insert Chapter — The Phone Call He Didn't Make

Skyler discovered Thorne had lied about a former employer.

Not exaggerated.

Lied.

Thorne had claimed influence over a contract termination that records later showed resulted from an unrelated budget cut decided before any known contact with the network.

Skyler felt rage.

Then relief.

Then rage at the relief.

He wanted to call the former manager.

He imagined the conversation.

**See? I know what happened now.**

But what had happened?

The manager had ended a contract for budget reasons.

Skyler had spent years privately suspecting the manager of collusion.

The person deserved an apology, not vindication.

He found the number.

Did not call.

Why?

Because apology might reopen something the other person had no interest in revisiting.

Skyler wrote an email instead.

Short.

No conspiracy history.

No demand for response.

> I revisited some old events and realized I may have interpreted the end of our work relationship through assumptions that were not supported. I was under significant stress, but that does not make those assumptions fair to you. No response is required. I wanted to acknowledge it.

He stared at **Send**.

This felt more vulnerable than publishing accusations.

Accusations preserved the self as injured.

Apologies admitted the self had also acted.

He sent it.

No response came.

That was allowed.

Two weeks later, a simple reply:

**Appreciate the note. Wish you well.**

Nothing else.

Skyler read it twice.

No reconciliation.

No friendship.

No dramatic clearing of the record.

Just a square on the board no longer occupied by an invented enemy.

That was enough.

## Insert Chapter — The Public Defender of Nobody

Online supporters became a new danger after the dossier gained attention.

People who believed Skyler had been targeted began defending claims Skyler himself no longer made.

One account posted:

**EVERY JOB LOSS WAS PART OF THE OPERATION. WAKE UP.**

Skyler replied:

**Evidence doesn't support that. Some events remain unrelated or unresolved.**

The supporter answered:

**They got to you.**

Skyler stared.

The belief system had become self-protecting.

If Skyler confirmed it, proof.

If he denied it, proof of coercion.

If he narrowed it, proof of compromise.

He recognized the architecture from the fake inheritance.

Narrative trap.

Another supporter insisted Lena was part of the secret society because she refused to publish children's photographs.

Skyler blocked the account.

A third accused Hanna of being a handler.

Blocked.

A fourth claimed Reyes was covering up the government's role.

Skyler did not block immediately.

He wrote:

**There is evidence of misconduct by specific people, including misuse of government-adjacent access. There is not evidence that an entire agency or government directed the campaign. Precision protects the case.**

The response:

**That's exactly what they told you to say.**

Skyler laughed.

Then blocked.

He learned that being supported could feel as dehumanizing as being attacked when supporters needed him to remain the character in their story.

Hero.

Chosen One.

Victim.

Whistleblower.

Nobody asked whether the role fit that day.

Skyler began using a sentence he hated because it disappointed everyone equally:

**I don't know enough to say that.**

It became his shield.

Not knowing was no longer surrender.

It was refusal to counterfeit certainty.

## Insert Chapter — The Children's Section Is Redacted

Lena placed a black rectangle across an entire paragraph.

Skyler objected.

"That's the emotional center."

"Exactly."

"People need to understand what it cost."

"They can understand without the details."

The paragraph described a daughter's reaction to a family event.

No crime.

No identifying public record.

Just a small private moment that made Skyler's fear legible.

It was beautiful writing.

It was also hers.

Not his alone.

Skyler knew that intellectually.

Writers were thieves by default unless ethics intervened.

"What if I anonymize?"

"People who know your family will still know."

"Change the detail?"

"Then you're fictionalizing a child's experience to strengthen your case."

He sighed.

"You enjoy removing my best material."

"Your children are not material."

The sentence ended the argument.

Skyler deleted the paragraph.

The chapter became weaker as propaganda and stronger as fatherhood.

That was the trade.

Later, when readers complained that the book refused to reveal certain details, Skyler would consider that a success.

A story about consent could not consume people who never consented to become its evidence.

## Insert Chapter — Chess With Hanna

Hanna did not like chess.

That became one of Skyler's favorite things about her.

He tried teaching her anyway.

"This one is the queen."

"I know chess pieces."

"You said you didn't play."

"Not playing and never seeing a chessboard are different."

"Good distinction."

"Don't make this a lesson."

They began.

Hanna moved a pawn.

Skyler responded automatically.

She moved another edge pawn.

He winced.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You made a face."

"It's your game."

"What was wrong with it?"

"Weakens squares."

"Maybe I like weak squares."

"Nobody likes weak squares."

"I do now."

Skyler laughed.

Three moves later, Hanna moved her queen too early.

Skyler physically struggled not to comment.

"You look constipated."

"I'm respecting your autonomy."

"That's what that looks like?"

"Apparently."

She lost quickly.

"This game is stupid."

"You moved your queen six times."

"She can move anywhere. Why wouldn't I?"

Skyler stopped.

There it was.

Power tempted overuse because movement felt like control.

The queen was strongest and therefore easiest to expose if used to solve every problem.

Narrative power worked the same way.

Skyler had spent years moving the queen.

Big posts.

Big theories.

Big explanations.

Everywhere at once.

Thorne wanted him to keep doing it.

Hanna reset the pieces.

"Again?"

Skyler smiled.

"Yeah."

"No advice unless I ask."

"That's painful."

"Boundary."

"Fine."

This time she developed a knight.

Skyler said nothing.

He was learning.
