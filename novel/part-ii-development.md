# PART II — DEVELOPMENT
## Bring Every Piece Into Play

> *A chess position becomes dangerous before the attack is visible. Development is only the act of putting pieces where they can later matter.*

## Chapter Seven
### The Breakup

The fight did not begin with shouting.

That was one of the lies memory told afterward.

When Skyler remembered the breakup, his mind wanted to cut directly to doors closing, accusations, phones lighting up, somebody saying *fine* in the tone that meant nothing was fine. Memory preferred explosions because explosions gave a scene borders.

The real beginning was quieter.

A load of laundry sat half-folded on the couch.

The television was on without either of them watching it.

One of the girls had left a plastic cup beneath the coffee table. Blue. Clouded with fingerprints. A little milk drying in the bottom.

Skyler's laptop was open on his knees.

His fiancée stood in the kitchen doorway.

"Are you working?"

"School."

"You said that an hour ago."

"It's still school an hour later."

She did not laugh.

Skyler looked up.

There were ways couples learned to recognize weather in each other's faces. A tightness around the mouth. A particular stillness. The refusal to sit down.

He closed the laptop.

"What's wrong?"

"Nothing."

That meant the list was long.

"Okay."

"You always say okay like you're trying to close a ticket."

"I don't know what I'm supposed to say when you say nothing."

"You're supposed to know it's not nothing."

"Then don't say nothing."

Her eyes flashed.

There it was.

Opening move.

Years later, Skyler would become obsessed with the idea that every conflict had a hidden first move. A private message. A rumor. A person behind the person. A pressure point someone else had touched.

But couples did not need secret societies to ruin an evening.

Sometimes two exhausted people could do it with twelve words.

She walked to the counter and picked up the mail.

"Did you call them?"

"Who?"

"The insurance people."

Skyler rubbed his forehead.

He had not.

"I'll call tomorrow."

"You said that yesterday."

"I had work."

"And school. And code. And chess. And whatever else is always more urgent until I bring something up."

"That's not fair."

"It doesn't have to be fair to be true."

The sentence hit him harder than it should have.

He opened the laptop again, not because he wanted to work, but because a screen gave his hands somewhere to go.

"There," she said. "That."

"What?"

"You hide in it."

"I'm not hiding."

"Skyler, you can be in the same room and be gone."

From the hallway, a child's voice called, "Mom?"

Both adults froze.

The argument changed temperature instantly.

"It's okay," she called back.

"Can I have water?"

Skyler stood.

"I'll get it."

He walked into the kitchen, filled a cup, and carried it down the hall.

The blue cup remained under the coffee table.

He did not notice it.

He would notice it years later in a photograph and believe, for several terrible hours, that its presence proved someone had been inside his home.

He would be wrong about that.

But on this night the cup was only a cup, and the thing destroying the room was not a hidden network.

It was accumulated disappointment.

The child took the water.

"Are you mad?"

Skyler crouched.

"No."

Children had a way of making adults lie for noble reasons.

"Go back to bed."

"Promise?"

"Promise."

When he returned to the living room, his fiancée had folded three shirts.

Not angrily.

That was worse.

"I don't want to do this anymore," she said.

Skyler stood in the doorway.

He felt the sentence before he understood it.

"Do what?"

She looked at him.

"This."

There was no music.

No dark sedan.

No anonymous message.

No man named Thorne waiting in a warehouse.

No organization arranging the board.

There was only the television talking to nobody, the laundry, the old laptop, the cup under the table, and the oldest terror in human life:

Someone you love deciding that love is no longer enough.

Skyler did what he always did when confronted with a system failure.

He began debugging.

"Okay, what's the actual issue?"

She laughed once in disbelief.

"The actual issue?"

"I mean, what needs to change?"

"You think there's a setting."

"No."

"You think I can give you requirements."

"I am trying to understand."

"That's the problem. You don't listen until something becomes a problem you can understand."

The sentence was unfair.

It was also close enough to true that defending himself would have sounded ridiculous.

Skyler sat.

For once he did not answer immediately.

She kept folding.

One shirt.

Another.

Then one of his.

She stopped with it in her hands.

"I don't hate you," she said.

"That's a low bar."

"Don't."

"I'm sorry."

"You make jokes when you're scared."

"I make jokes all the time."

"Exactly."

He looked down.

The relationship had been accumulating cracks for longer than either wanted to admit. Money. Trust. Time. Jealousy. Work. Exhaustion. Mistakes that became arguments and arguments that became personality traits.

There were things Skyler believed she had done to him.

There were things she believed he had done to her.

Some could be documented.

Some depended on whose memory entered the room first.

The book could have made one of them the villain.

That would have been easier.

But life rarely gave adults that mercy.

"What happens with the girls?" Skyler asked.

That was the question beneath every other question.

Her face changed.

"We figure it out."

"Together?"

"As parents."

"That's not what I meant."

"I know."

He stared at the closed laptop.

A tiny notification light blinked at the edge.

For a second he hated it.

The machines, the deadlines, the repositories, the half-built systems, the constant feeling that his real life would begin after he finished the next thing.

What if this had been the real life?

What if he had optimized around the only part that mattered?

"Is there somebody else?" he asked.

Silence.

Not long.

Long enough.

Skyler's body reacted before his mind formed a theory.

Heat in his face.

Pressure in his chest.

A metallic taste.

"Who?"

"It isn't that simple."

"That means yes."

"I didn't say yes."

"You didn't say no."

"This is why I didn't want to do this tonight."

"Do what? Tell me there's somebody else?"

"I said it isn't that simple."

The conversation accelerated.

Questions became accusations. Accusations became old history. Old history became evidence that each of them had apparently been collecting without knowing they were building cases.

By midnight, Skyler had committed three mistakes that would become habits later.

He demanded certainty before anyone in the room possessed it.

He treated contradiction as proof of deception.

And he believed that if he could ask the correct sequence of questions, pain would eventually yield a clean answer.

It did not.

At 12:41, he stepped outside.

Arkansas air hung warm and wet around the porch.

A dog barked three houses away.

Somebody's television flashed blue through blinds across the street.

Skyler's phone buzzed.

A stupid promotional notification.

**READY FOR A FRESH START?**

He stared at it.

Then laughed.

"Oh, fuck you."

He deleted it.

That was years before he began wondering whether advertisements were speaking to him personally.

Back then, he still understood that algorithms were capable of cruel timing without possessing intent.

He sat on the porch step.

Inside, the person he thought he would marry was deciding where she would sleep.

Down the hall, three girls were learning without knowing it that a house could remain standing while a family changed shape inside it.

Skyler put his face in his hands.

The opening was over.

Neither player had noticed.

## Chapter Eight
### The Quiet House

The first night alone was not lonely.

It was administrative.

Skyler spent two hours moving objects because he did not know what else to do with his hands.

He threw away food nobody would eat now.

He collected hair ties from surfaces.

He found a tiny sock beneath the couch.

He discovered the blue cup.

The milk inside had turned.

He washed it and set it upside down beside the sink.

Then he stood in the kitchen and listened.

The house made noises he had never heard while the girls were there.

The refrigerator clicked.

The air conditioner whispered in the vents.

A branch touched the gutter.

The building had not become haunted.

It had become audible.

Skyler walked into the girls' room.

Blankets.

A stuffed animal face-down near the wall.

A plastic bracelet.

A marker without a cap.

The emotional violence of absence was embarrassingly ordinary.

Nothing had been destroyed.

That was what destroyed him.

He sat on the edge of a bed and picked up the stuffed animal.

"This is pathetic," he told it.

The animal declined comment.

His phone showed twelve notifications.

Two from school.

One from work.

One bank alert.

Three messages he did not want to answer.

A chess app reminding him that his daily puzzle was ready.

A social-media recommendation titled **People You May Know**.

He opened the chess puzzle.

The board appeared.

White to move.

A queen sacrifice.

Skyler stared at the position and almost threw the phone.

"Subtle."

He solved it anyway.

Queen takes h7.

King takes.

Knight g5.

The forced sequence arrived several moves later.

He played it twice.

The beauty of chess was that sacrifice could be objectively correct.

Life did not annotate itself afterward.

No engine appeared over a breakup saying **+4.7**.

No arrow showed which sentence should have been spoken.

No evaluation bar explained whether leaving was brave or stupid.

There was only time.

And time was a terrible analyst because it kept changing the position.

Skyler opened a notes app.

He wrote:

**Things I need to fix**

1. Money.
2. School.
3. Work.
4. House.
5. Relationship with kids.
6. Me.

He stared at number six.

Then added:

7. Everything else.

That was more honest.

For the next several weeks he lived like a man trying to outwork grief.

He accepted shifts.

He submitted assignments late and then early and then at 2:00 a.m.

He wrote code no one had asked for.

He renamed folders.

He played chess until the board stopped looking like sixty-four squares and became pure movement.

He downloaded a Mandarin app because learning tones gave his brain a puzzle with no emotional history.

First tone: high and level.

Second: rising.

Third: dipping.

Fourth: falling hard.

Same syllable.

Different meaning.

He liked that.

It felt honest.

Meaning depended on context.

Context could change everything.

One night he practiced *ma* until the app became cruel.

"Wrong tone," it told him.

"I know."

"Try again."

"I am."

"Let's practice."

"You don't know what I'm going through."

The cartoon mascot smiled.

Skyler laughed for the first time that day.

He sent the girls a voice message.

"I'm learning Chinese."

Luna replied fifteen minutes later.

"Why?"

He recorded another.

"Because apparently English wasn't enough trouble."

She sent back laughter.

He saved it.

Not because he expected to need evidence that his daughter once laughed.

Because the house was quiet and the sound changed its shape.

The archive began innocently.

That mattered.

Later, people would see folders filled with screenshots, recordings, dates, notes, copies, exported messages, logs, photographs, and assume the collection itself proved obsession.

Sometimes it did.

But the first archive was a father saving a laugh.

A school email.

A photograph.

A chess game.

A text he did not want to lose when he changed phones.

Human beings had been archiving love long before hard drives.

The danger came when Skyler stopped distinguishing memory from evidence.

He crossed that line gradually.

Nobody heard it break.

## Chapter Nine
### Two Bad Ideas

Grief and ego make an efficient dating service.

That was how Skyler would explain it later.

At the time he described it differently.

"I'm moving on."

His friend Marcus looked across the table at him.

"You're doing what?"

"Moving on."

"You downloaded three dating apps yesterday."

"Efficiently moving on."

"You still have family pictures on your lock screen."

Skyler looked at the phone.

"They're my kids. I'm not moving on from being a dad."

"You know what I mean."

They sat in a restaurant where the televisions showed different sports and none had sound. Skyler kept glancing at one screen because a chess tournament graphic had appeared between commercials.

Marcus snapped his fingers.

"You're not listening."

"I am."

"What did I say?"

"Something judgmental."

"I said don't date anybody if you're trying to punish somebody else."

Skyler leaned back.

"I'm not punishing anybody."

"Then why did you tell me how hot they both are before telling me either one's name?"

Skyler smiled despite himself.

"Because they are."

Marcus covered his face.

"We're doomed."

The first woman danced at a club outside town.

The second did too.

Skyler found this funny in a way that should have warned him about his own motives.

There was nothing inherently scandalous about their work. The problem was that Skyler was using the fact as narrative ammunition in a private war his ex-fiancée had not agreed to fight.

He imagined people hearing about it.

He imagined surprise.

Jealousy.

A message traveling back.

He told himself he did not care whether it did.

Then he selected a shirt based on whether the photograph might look good online.

That should have settled the question.

The dates were not cinematic.

One woman hated onions and picked them out of a burger with surgical concentration.

The other had an elderly dog and showed Skyler fourteen photographs before asking anything about him.

Both were funnier than he expected.

Both were more complicated than the role he had assigned them in his head.

That embarrassed him.

People refused to remain symbols if you listened long enough.

On the second date, the woman with the dog asked, "So what's your deal?"

"My deal?"

"Everybody has one."

"I'm in school. I work. I build software. I have three daughters."

"That's your résumé."

"I play chess."

"Still résumé."

"I'm recently single."

"There it is."

He looked down.

"That obvious?"

"You mention your ex every eight minutes while insisting you're over your ex."

"I have not mentioned her that much."

She raised an eyebrow.

Skyler checked the conversation mentally.

He had.

"Damn."

"You seem nice."

"That sounded like a sentence before bad news."

"You also seem like you're trying to win something nobody else is playing."

The phrase landed.

He thought of chess.

"That's annoyingly specific."

"Tip your waitress."

"I'm going to."

"Then we're good."

They laughed.

Nothing sinister happened.

Nobody recruited him.

Nobody planted a message.

Nobody photographed the table from a surveillance van.

Later, after the strange events began, Skyler would revisit this period and search it for origins. A stranger who looked too long. A car parked twice in the same place. A question that seemed unusually informed.

That was the danger of retrospective fear.

It converted ordinary scenes into auditions for later meaning.

But there was one event from those weeks he never fully explained.

It happened at a gas station after midnight.

Skyler had stopped for an energy drink because he was driving home after seeing the second woman. The store was empty except for the clerk and a man near the lottery display.

Skyler bought the drink.

As he turned to leave, the man said, "Careful moving two queens at once."

Skyler stopped.

"What?"

The man did not look at him.

"Chess."

"Do I know you?"

"No."

"Then why'd you say that?"

The man scratched a ticket with a coin.

"Your shirt."

Skyler looked down.

He was wearing a chess-club T-shirt.

A knight and queen were printed across the chest.

He laughed.

"Right."

The man looked up then.

"Two queens'll get you killed."

"Only if you promote a pawn."

"Or if you think you're the king."

Skyler left.

For months, the exchange meant nothing.

Years later, when someone used the phrase **two queens** in an anonymous message, the memory returned with enough force to feel like proof.

It was not proof.

But neither could he establish that the repetition was random.

The position acquired another piece.

## Chapter Ten
### Seven Jobs

The number seven became mythology because seven sounded designed.

Seven days.

Seven sins.

Seven seals.

Seven jobs.

In reality, employment loss did not occur in a perfect sequence. Some positions overlapped. Some were short contracts. Some ended for reasons so ordinary they resisted storytelling. A schedule conflict. A manager who disliked him. A role that never became permanent. A mistake. A misunderstanding. A company changing plans.

Others felt stranger.

That distinction mattered.

Skyler learned to separate them too late.

### I. The Nursery

Dirt made sense.

That was one reason he liked nursery work.

A plant did not care what somebody had posted about you.

A pallet did not read screenshots.

A tree either fit in the truck or did not.

On hot days, sweat darkened the back of his shirt before nine in the morning. His hands accumulated tiny cuts. Soil embedded beneath his nails no matter how hard he scrubbed.

One afternoon, a customer pointed toward a row of half-brown shrubs.

"Those dead?"

Skyler crouched and snapped a twig.

Green inside.

"Dormant."

"Looks dead."

"That's not the same thing."

The customer shrugged.

Skyler stared at the green tissue inside the broken branch.

Looks dead.

Not the same thing.

He carried the sentence home.

The nursery did not end dramatically. Hours changed. Life changed. Work shifted.

No conspiracy required.

### II. The Factory

Factory noise entered the body.

Machines hammered rhythm into the floor. Forklifts beeped. Compressed air hissed. People learned to speak with eyebrows because words disappeared under equipment.

Skyler became a team lead.

The title sounded more important outside the building than inside it.

Inside it meant somebody called your name when a line stopped.

"Skyler!"

He looked up.

A worker pointed toward a jam.

Skyler walked over.

"What happened?"

"It hates me."

"Machine doesn't know you."

"Then why's it personal?"

Skyler smiled.

They cleared it.

He liked systems when failure could be traced.

Sensor.

Material.

Timing.

Human error.

Fix the condition, restart the line.

Relationships and institutions were not like that.

He wished they were.

His factory job eventually ended in the normal brutality of employment: a person can be necessary on Monday and absent from the schedule later without the universe acknowledging the transition.

But one conversation stayed with him.

A supervisor called him into an office.

"People say you've got a lot going on."

Skyler sat.

"Everybody has a lot going on."

"You know what I mean."

He did not.

"No."

The supervisor tapped a pen against the desk.

"Personal stuff."

Skyler felt irritation rise.

"What personal stuff?"

"I'm not getting into rumors."

"Then why bring them up?"

"Because perception matters when you're leading people."

There it was.

Perception.

A word that would follow him for years.

"What exactly is the perception?"

"Don't do this."

"Do what? Ask what you're accusing me of?"

"I'm not accusing you of anything."

"Then we're talking about nothing."

The supervisor sighed.

"This is what I mean."

Skyler left the office with no new facts and a new fear.

Somewhere, people were talking.

That was ordinary.

What he did with that uncertainty later would not be.

### III. The Contract

The first software contract he lost hurt worse than the factory.

Software was supposed to be proof.

Code either ran or it did not.

Tests passed or failed.

A repository contained what it contained.

At least that was the religion Skyler wanted engineering to be.

The real workplace included humans.

A client scheduled a call.

The client sounded uncomfortable.

"We're pausing the engagement."

"Why?"

"Internal review."

"Of what?"

"Access and fit."

"Access to what?"

"Skyler."

His name, spoken that way, became a warning.

"I need more than that."

"I can't provide more than that."

"Did I do something?"

"We're not making a finding."

"Then why am I losing the contract?"

"We're pausing it."

"Without paying me?"

Silence.

"Right," Skyler said.

Afterward he opened every account log he could legally access.

Nothing obvious.

No failed authentication.

No strange device.

No malicious commit.

No reason.

He made his first serious evidence folder.

`CONTRACT_REVIEW`

Inside:

- emails
- invoices
- access notices
- screenshots
- calendar records
- notes from calls

He did not know what he expected to discover.

Maybe consistency.

Instead he found human ambiguity preserved perfectly in digital form.

### IV. The Interview

A recruiter loved him on Tuesday.

By Thursday, she did not answer.

On Friday, she sent:

**We have decided to move forward with other candidates.**

Ordinary.

Painfully ordinary.

Skyler almost filed it in the archive anyway.

Then he stopped.

"No," he said aloud.

Not every no belonged in a conspiracy folder.

He deleted the screenshot.

That decision became important later because it proved he was still capable of subtraction.

### V. The Manager Who Used the Phrase

At another job, a manager said, "You can't build your way out of everything."

Skyler froze.

The phrase meant something only because he had seen nearly identical wording in an advertisement months earlier.

"What did you say?"

The manager looked confused.

"I said you can't solve a people problem by writing another tool."

"No, the first part."

"Skyler, are you okay?"

He felt the room tilt—not literally, but socially.

A small question could change how a person looked at you.

"Yeah."

"You sure?"

"Where'd you hear that phrase?"

"What phrase?"

"Build your way out of everything."

The manager laughed.

"I don't know. I just said it."

Skyler searched the web later.

Thousands of similar phrases.

Nothing unique.

He marked the event **UNRESOLVED / LOW CONFIDENCE**.

That was discipline.

Fear hated discipline.

### VI. The Security Review

This one was different.

A security officer contacted him about an account login.

"Did you sign in from Little Rock at 3:11 a.m.?"

Skyler sat upright.

"No."

"Were you using a VPN?"

"Not then."

"Any shared device?"

"No."

The officer reset his access.

For the first time, there was a concrete anomaly.

A record.

A location.

A time.

Skyler asked for a copy.

"We don't release raw logs."

"Can you confirm it in writing?"

Pause.

"I can confirm the security review occurred."

"And the login?"

"I can say there was activity requiring verification."

"That's not the same thing."

"It's what I can say."

Skyler wrote every word down.

He began to understand something about institutions.

They could possess certainty privately and communicate ambiguity publicly for liability, policy, privacy, or simple caution.

To the person outside, that could feel indistinguishable from concealment.

Sometimes it was concealment.

Sometimes it was procedure.

Sometimes both.

The account event was the first square on the board Skyler could point to and say:

Something happened here.

He still could not say who moved the piece.

### VII. The Job That Never Started

The last one hurt because it offered rescue first.

A remote role.

Decent pay.

Technical work.

A new beginning delivered by email.

Skyler completed paperwork.

He told his mother.

He told a friend.

He nearly told the girls but decided to wait until the first paycheck.

Then the company delayed onboarding.

A week.

Then another.

Finally:

**We are unable to proceed at this time.**

He called.

Nobody returned it.

He emailed.

A generic response.

He checked the company.

Real.

He checked the recruiter.

Real profile.

He checked the domain.

Real.

He checked himself.

That was the dangerous part.

What was wrong with him that jobs kept dissolving?

The mind prefers an enemy to randomness because enemies can be fought.

Skyler's first theory was not a secret society.

It was a blacklist.

Some database.

Some report.

Some background service.

Some person whispering to the next person.

He had no proof.

But unlike the advertisements and phrases, the employment pattern had consequences that accumulated in bank statements.

He created a spreadsheet.

Employer.

Date.

Reason given.

Documents.

Known contacts.

Possible links.

Confidence.

The spreadsheet contained no grand revelation.

Yet.

At the bottom he added one cell in bold:

**Do not turn repetition into causation.**

Then beneath it:

**Do not ignore repetition either.**

That contradiction became the operating system of his fear.

## Interlude
### The Government Gangsters

The phrase first appeared in a notebook as a joke.

Not a public accusation.

Not a manifesto.

A joke.

Skyler had spent the afternoon trying to obtain a document from an office that told him another office had it. The second office told him the first office had it. A third person said the document probably existed but could not explain where.

On the drive home, he said to himself:

"Government gangsters."

He laughed.

It was the kind of phrase a frustrated person invented because bureaucracy felt personal when you were trapped inside it.

That night he wrote:

**GOVERNMENT GANGSTERS = people with official power acting like street criminals**

Then beneath it:

**Not 'the government.' Individuals. Important difference.**

The distinction would save him later.

Because eventually he would encounter people who seemed to carry information they should not have, confidence they had not earned, and language that sounded borrowed from institutions.

Some would be frauds.

Some would be contractors.

Some would be criminals playing dress-up.

At least one would have access that turned out to be real.

But on the night Skyler invented the phrase, nobody was following him.

Nobody was in the driveway.

Nobody had tapped the notebook.

He was a tired man angry at paperwork.

The danger came later, when the joke started feeling predictive.

# PART III — THE PIN

## Chapter Eleven
### The Feed

The feed changed before Skyler admitted that he had noticed.

At first it was only density.

One fatherhood video became five.

One story about hacked accounts became an hour of cybercrime clips.

One chess post became grandmasters, sacrifices, traps, zugzwang, poisoned pawns.

One article about custody disputes became a river of family-court arguments.

Algorithms did that.

Skyler knew algorithms did that.

He had spent enough time building recommendation-adjacent software to understand the basic economics of attention.

Click one thing.

Get more of the thing.

Pause on another.

Get adjacent things.

Comment angrily.

Congratulations: the machine has learned that anger retains you.

What bothered him was not that the feed reflected his interests.

What bothered him was timing.

On a Wednesday morning, he drafted a private note:

**Stop moving every piece because you can.**

The line came from the shelter chess player years earlier.

He had never posted it, as far as he remembered.

At lunch, his feed recommended a chess video titled:

**STOP MOVING EVERY PIECE**

Skyler stared.

Then he did the disciplined thing.

He searched the phrase.

Common chess advice.

Not unique.

He marked it coincidence.

That evening, he argued with someone by text and typed:

**I feel like everybody wants me to play a role in a story I didn't write.**

He did not send it.

He deleted the draft.

Thirty minutes later, an advertisement appeared:

**STOP PLAYING A ROLE. WRITE YOUR OWN STORY.**

Skyler put the phone down.

Still generic.

Painfully generic.

Marketing had been using that language since before smartphones.

He knew that.

His nervous system did not care.

The next morning, the feed served a video called **WHEN YOUR PHONE KNOWS TOO MUCH**.

Skyler laughed aloud.

"Okay."

He did not click it.

The thumbnail remained on-screen.

A cracked mirror.

Blue.

He had seen similar artwork in a music recommendation weeks earlier.

Again: not proof.

He began taking screenshots.

Not because each event mattered.

Because he wanted to know whether the feeling of repetition survived contact with a record.

Most of it did not.

This discovery should have comforted him.

It did, briefly.

Out of forty-seven screenshots collected across ten days, thirty-one became uninteresting when reviewed later.

Generic language.

Topics he had searched.

Ads explainable by obvious data.

Repeated imagery common across design trends.

Seven were odd but plausible.

Six he could not classify.

Three bothered him.

The first was a photograph of a dark-blue sedan matching a car he had once owned.

Common car.

The second was a music title matching a phrase from a private draft.

Possible cloud leakage, prior exposure, coincidence.

The third was a comment under a public post from an empty account:

**YOUR QUEEN IS ON VACATION AND YOUR KING IS NAKED.**

Skyler did not breathe for several seconds.

The shelter chess player had said those exact words years earlier.

At least, Skyler remembered them as exact.

Memory was the first problem.

Had he posted the story somewhere?

Possibly.

Had he told it to friends?

Definitely.

Could someone who knew him be messing with him?

Absolutely.

Could he be remembering the old sentence more precisely because the new one gave it shape?

Yes.

But the account bothered him.

No posts.

No avatar.

No followers.

Created recently.

He took screenshots.

Copied the profile link.

Wrote the time.

Then, against every instinct, he did not reply.

An hour later the account vanished.

That was the first move Skyler could not easily put back in the box.

## Chapter Twelve
### Convenience Store

The store was too bright.

That was Skyler's first thought.

Not threatening.

Bright.

Fluorescent light flattened every object—the chip bags, lottery signs, cooler doors, the tired face of the clerk behind the counter.

Skyler entered for coffee and gasoline.

He had slept four hours.

The previous night he had spent too long comparing screenshots that did not become clearer through repetition.

A song played quietly above the coolers.

He did not notice it until the chorus.

A male voice sang:

> You keep the receipts like they owe you a name.

Skyler looked up.

The lyric hit too directly.

Then the next line rhymed *name* with *game*, and the spell weakened.

Lots of songs used receipts metaphorically.

He poured coffee.

The song continued.

> Seven doors, seven jobs, everybody's to blame.

Skyler stopped pouring.

Coffee overflowed onto his hand.

"Shit."

He set the cup down.

Seven jobs.

The phrase was not public, he thought.

Then corrected himself.

He had complained about job losses online.

Had he used the number seven?

Maybe.

He opened his phone.

No signal inside the store.

The clerk looked over.

"You good?"

"What's this song?"

The clerk listened.

"No idea."

"Who controls the music?"

"Corporate playlist."

"Can you see the track?"

The clerk stared.

"Man, I just work here."

Skyler almost laughed.

"Fair."

He walked outside.

A car passed slowly near the pumps, bass audible through closed windows.

Different song.

For four seconds, Skyler heard a voice repeat:

> Everybody wants the chosen one until it's time to choose.

The car turned onto the road.

Skyler stood still.

Chosen one.

He had used that phrase jokingly in an unpublished book title draft.

Had he?

Or had the phrase existed in his head because he had heard something like this before?

He hated that question.

His phone regained signal.

A recommendation notification appeared:

**THE CHOSEN ONE — new track you may like**

Skyler's heart accelerated.

Three channels.

Store song.

Passing car.

Phone.

Same conceptual phrase.

Within minutes.

He took screenshots.

Then forced himself to write alternative explanations before leaving the parking lot.

Store playlist may include popular track.

Passing car may be unrelated.

Recommendation may respond to music metadata if device recognized ambient audio.

"Can phones do that?" he whispered.

He searched.

Some services could identify songs. Some microphones listened for wake words. Ad-tech ecosystems shared behavior in ways ordinary users rarely understood.

Possible.

Not comforting.

A man exited the store wearing a cracked-mirror logo on his shirt.

Skyler's stomach dropped.

The man walked toward a pickup.

"Hey," Skyler called.

The man turned.

"Where'd you get that shirt?"

He looked down.

"Concert."

"What concert?"

"Why?"

"I've seen that symbol."

The man's face hardened.

"Okay."

Skyler heard himself from outside his body.

A stranger asking about symbols in a gas-station parking lot.

He changed tone immediately.

"Sorry. I like the design."

The man shrugged.

"Band's called Mirror Room."

Skyler went cold.

"What?"

"Mirror Room."

The man got into his truck.

Skyler searched the band.

Real.

Small regional act.

Formed years earlier.

Cracked mirror logo.

Nothing secret.

Nothing connected to him.

He stood beside the pump and laughed once, shakily.

There was the lesson.

A symbol could feel like a revelation and turn out to be merchandise.

Then he saw the band's newest single title.

**GOVERNMENT GANGSTERS**

Skyler's laughter stopped.

The song had been uploaded that morning.

He had never published the phrase.

He was almost certain.

Almost.

The word *almost* became the most dangerous word in his life.

## Chapter Thirteen
### Four Notes

Skyler listened to the track once.

Then again.

Then he hated himself and listened a third time.

The song was not about him.

At least not explicitly.

It described corrupt officials, criminals in suits, badges rented to private money, contractors who knew more than they should, and people who acted powerful because nobody could tell which parts of their authority were real.

The phrase *government gangsters* appeared only in the chorus.

Generic enough to exist independently.

Skyler searched exact matches.

The phrase had been used before by commentators, activists, criminals, journalists, angry defendants, musicians, and ordinary people furious at institutions.

His private notebook did not own two common words.

He knew that.

What bothered him came at the end.

Four notes.

Low.

High.

Lower.

Same high note again.

He had heard the pattern in the instrumental track titled **THE MAN WHO BUILT TOO MANY DOORS**.

He pulled up the earlier audio.

Played both.

Again.

Same sequence.

Maybe common.

Four notes allowed enormous coincidence.

He needed someone else to hear it without context.

He sent both tracks to Marcus.

**Do these have anything in common?**

Marcus called instead of texting.

"This a trap?"

"No."

"Because if this is another relationship question disguised as music—"

"Listen to the endings."

Silence on the line.

Skyler waited.

"Okay."

"What?"

"Same little piano thing."

Skyler sat straighter.

"Exactly."

"So?"

"Different artists. Different platforms. Different months."

"Samples exist."

"Can you search it?"

"No, because unlike you, I enjoy having a life."

"Marcus."

"Yes, it's the same. No, that doesn't mean the CIA wrote a mixtape about you."

Skyler laughed despite himself.

"I didn't say CIA."

"You were thinking something with letters."

"I was thinking somebody's reusing an audio signature."

"That is a normal thought. Stay there."

The four notes became the first mystery another person independently noticed.

That mattered.

Not because it proved a conspiracy.

Because it proved Skyler had not imagined the similarity.

He created a new folder:

`FOUR_NOTES`

Then, after staring at it, renamed the folder:

`AUDIO_COMPARISON_UNRESOLVED`

The second name was uglier.

It was also better.

Truth often had worse branding.

## Chapter Fourteen
### The Sermon

Church had once been one of the few places Skyler tried not to analyze.

He was bad at it.

He noticed microphone feedback, projector delays, exit placement, whether the Wi-Fi name had changed, which songs produced the loudest singing, who sat where, who left early, who avoided whom after service.

But he tried.

Faith was difficult enough without turning God into a dataset.

On Sunday, he sat near the back.

His mother sat beside him.

"You look tired," she whispered.

"That's because I am."

"You sleeping?"

"Sometimes."

She gave him the look mothers reserve for adult sons who believe adulthood revoked jurisdiction.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You taught me that means something."

"Good. You're learning."

The pastor spoke about control.

Ordinary sermon topic.

"Some of you," he said, "are trying to solve spiritual pain by collecting more information."

Skyler's attention sharpened.

His mother shifted beside him.

The pastor continued.

"You think if you can know who said what, why this happened, what that person intended, what door closed, what message meant what—you'll finally have peace."

Skyler stopped breathing normally.

The words aligned too well with his week.

He looked around.

Nobody looked at him.

"But information is not the same as wisdom," the pastor said. "And suspicion is not discernment."

Skyler felt heat rise in his face.

Suspicion.

Discernment.

Had somebody talked to the pastor?

His mother touched his arm.

"Don't," she whispered.

"Don't what?"

"Make this about you."

The sentence irritated him because it arrived before he spoke.

"I'm not."

She looked at him.

He knew the look.

After service, Skyler waited until the room thinned.

Then he approached the pastor.

"Can I ask you something?"

"Sure."

"Where'd today's sermon come from?"

The pastor smiled cautiously.

"Long week and an old passage. Why?"

"Anybody talk to you about me?"

Pause.

Small.

But Skyler saw it.

"People talk to me about lots of people," the pastor said.

"Recently. About me."

"Skyler, if somebody speaks to me in confidence, I can't—"

"So yes."

"That's not what I said."

"It's what you didn't say."

The pastor's expression changed.

"You need to be careful doing that."

"Doing what?"

"Turning boundaries into admissions."

The sentence was good.

Skyler hated it.

"Was the sermon directed at me?"

"No."

The answer came immediately.

"Any part?"

"The sermon was directed at the congregation. If part of it applies to you, maybe listen to that part."

"Did somebody tell you I'm collecting information?"

"You post about screenshots."

Skyler blinked.

Of course.

He had.

Publicly.

Not everything he felt was private actually was.

The realization embarrassed him.

Then the pastor added, "And somebody did mention you were going through a hard time."

There.

Both explanations.

Public information and private conversation.

Nothing sinister.

Yet Skyler walked to the parking lot feeling watched because ambiguity, once activated, did not care whether the explanation was benign.

His mother caught up.

"You can't interrogate church."

"I asked a question."

"You asked six."

"He answered weird."

"People answer weird when you question them like they're on trial."

Skyler unlocked the car.

"What if somebody is talking about me?"

"People are always talking about everybody."

"That's not reassuring."

"It's not supposed to be. It's supposed to be ordinary."

He looked at her.

She softened.

"Baby, ordinary things can hurt you too. You don't need somebody behind all of them."

Skyler wanted to argue.

Instead he got into the car.

On the passenger seat, his phone lit with a notification from the music app.

**New release from Mirror Room: ORDINARY THINGS CAN HURT YOU TOO**

Skyler stared.

His mother tapped on the window.

He lowered it.

"What?"

She saw his face.

"What happened?"

He turned the phone toward her.

The notification was gone.

He opened the app.

No such track appeared.

His mother waited.

"Nothing," he said.

The word sounded different now.

## Chapter Fifteen
### Good Rule

At 2:22 the next morning, Skyler received the message that changed the game.

He had been working on a repository README.

Not hacking.

Not investigating.

Documentation.

He wrote a sentence to himself in a private draft:

**Make the claim match the code.**

It was a professional rule he had been trying to adopt across his portfolio. Stop describing prototypes like finished products. Stop describing architecture like deployment. Stop confusing ambition with evidence.

He liked the sentence because it was uncomfortable.

Truth that flattered you was easy.

The useful kind usually took something away.

He saved the draft.

His phone buzzed.

Anonymous account.

No avatar.

No history.

One message:

**Good rule. Use it on your life.**

Skyler did not move.

The room became intensely specific.

Laptop fan.

Air vent.

Half-empty glass.

Black knight beside the keyboard.

The blue cup, now used for pens.

Phone in his left hand.

He took a screenshot.

Another.

Recorded the screen.

Opened the profile.

Blank.

He copied the URL.

Then he did something new.

He did not ask who they were.

He wrote:

**What claim?**

The typing indicator appeared.

Stopped.

Appeared again.

Then:

**That one player owns the board.**

Skyler's throat tightened.

He typed:

**I never said that.**

Response:

**Not publicly.**

He stood so quickly the chair rolled backward.

This was different.

Maybe.

He forced himself to list possibilities.

Someone with access to his drafts.

Someone he had spoken to.

Someone guessing.

Someone manipulating him using generic statements.

A friend.

A prank.

A compromised account.

A lie.

He typed:

**What do you want?**

The answer took almost a minute.

**For you to stop helping them.**

**Helping who?**

**The people who need you to believe they're gods.**

Skyler stared.

**Secret society?**

No response.

**Government?**

No response.

He almost typed *government gangsters*.

Stopped.

That phrase was the test.

If the account used it first, he would know something.

The message arrived:

**Cute phrase. Government gangsters. Keep it private.**

Skyler stood frozen.

He checked the notebook.

Physical notebook.

Not cloud.

Had he said the phrase aloud?

Yes.

Maybe to Marcus.

Maybe his mother.

Maybe in the car with voice assistant active.

Maybe on a call.

Maybe he had typed it somewhere and forgotten.

The fact that a phrase felt private did not make it private.

But this was the first event that frightened him enough to change behavior.

He covered the laptop camera.

Then felt stupid.

Then left it covered.

The account sent one final message.

**You think they're one thing. They're not. That's why you're losing.**

The profile disappeared.

Skyler sat in the quiet house.

On paper, he wrote three possibilities:

**A. SECRET SOCIETY**

**B. GOVERNMENT GANGSTERS**

**C. CRIMINALS PRETENDING TO BE BOTH**

Then a fourth:

**D. SOMEONE TRYING TO MAKE ME BELIEVE A–C**

He stared at the list.

Chess players knew the danger of seeing only candidate moves they wanted to play.

Sometimes the correct move was not on the first list.

Skyler added:

**E. I DON'T KNOW.**

That was the strongest move on the page.

He hated it.
