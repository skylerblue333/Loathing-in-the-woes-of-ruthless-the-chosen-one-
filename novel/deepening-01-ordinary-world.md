# DEEPENING CHAPTERS I
## Ordinary World — Insert Across Parts I and II

These chapters belong before the thriller fully activates. Their purpose is to make the reader experience the life the later story threatens, rather than hearing that life summarized after the fact.

---

## Insert Chapter — Sunday Shoes

Sunday mornings were military operations run by people too small to respect command.

Skyler discovered this while attempting to get three girls dressed for church.

Luna could not find the shoes she had been told not to remove from the place where shoes belonged.

Summer had found her shoes but rejected them on constitutional grounds.

Alexis wore one shoe and considered the problem solved.

"Nobody moves," Skyler said.

All three moved.

"Excellent. Great start."

The house looked like an evidence photograph after a very cheerful burglary. Clothes across the couch. Hairbrush on the kitchen counter. A cereal bowl beside a Bible. One small sock in the hallway with no known partner.

Skyler checked the time.

"We have twelve minutes."

"Until church?" Luna asked.

"Until I give up and tell God traffic was bad."

Summer laughed.

"That's lying."

"Correct. Which is why we're finding your shoes."

"I found them."

"You're not wearing them."

"I don't like them."

"You liked them yesterday."

"That was yesterday."

Skyler looked toward the ceiling.

"Fair argument. Terrible timing."

He had grown up thinking adulthood meant knowing what to do. Fatherhood revealed the scam. Adults were simply the people responsible for making decisions before adequate information became available.

Which shoes.

Which school.

Which job.

Which doctor.

How much discipline.

How much patience.

When to protect.

When to let a child fail safely.

Whether a cough was a cough or the beginning of something expensive.

Whether an argument needed intervention or siblings needed five minutes to discover diplomacy through mutual exhaustion.

No evaluation bar appeared afterward.

Church was one of the places Skyler went partly because he needed somewhere to admit that control had limits.

He was not always good at the admitting part.

They arrived seven minutes late.

Skyler considered that a victory.

The foyer smelled like coffee, perfume, old carpet, and somebody's breakfast sandwich.

A volunteer at the door smiled at the girls.

"Morning."

"We survived," Skyler said.

"Barely," Luna added.

The volunteer laughed.

"That kind of morning?"

"She lost her shoes," Summer said, immediately betraying her sister.

"I did not lose them."

"They were under your bed."

"I knew where they were."

"Then why didn't you get them?"

"I forgot where I knew."

Skyler covered his face.

"Can we enter the sanctuary before theology becomes impossible?"

They sat near the back.

Not because Skyler planned escape routes.

Because arriving late made the back practical.

Years later, fear would rewrite that preference. He would remember always sitting near exits and wonder whether some part of him had sensed danger before the danger existed.

That was memory's dishonest magic.

A practical habit became prophecy once the future needed an origin.

At the time, Skyler sat near the back because three children were likely to require bathroom trips, water, snacks, discipline, questions, or an emergency explanation of why whispering still counted as talking.

The pastor began.

Summer leaned toward Skyler.

"How long?"

"We just got here."

"I know. How long?"

"Until church is over?"

She nodded.

"An hour."

Her face collapsed.

"That's forever."

"You're eight. Everything is forever."

Luna whispered, "I'm hungry."

"You ate."

"That was before."

Skyler looked toward the pulpit.

"I am being tested."

An older woman in front of them turned and smiled.

Not annoyed.

Kind.

She reached into her purse and passed three wrapped peppermints backward.

Skyler mouthed thank you.

The girls became silent for almost two miraculous minutes.

The sermon was about seeds.

Small actions.

Hidden growth.

Faithfulness without immediate evidence.

The kind of message Skyler liked because it aligned nicely with software development if you forced the analogy hard enough.

Commit small changes.

Build over time.

Do not judge the repository by the first file.

He nearly wrote that down.

Then the pastor said, "Some of you are so busy building the future that you are absent from the life God already gave you."

Skyler stopped.

That one felt personal.

Not secretly targeted.

Personal because it applied.

He looked at the girls.

Summer was drawing on the bulletin.

Luna had arranged the peppermint wrappers into a tiny triangle.

Alexis leaned against his arm, warm and heavy.

Skyler closed the notes app.

For the rest of the sermon, he tried to be where he was.

Afterward, the church parking lot became its own ecosystem.

People stood beside vehicles talking long after the formal service ended. Children ran between adults with dangerous confidence. Somebody invited Skyler to lunch. Somebody else asked about work. An older man wanted to know whether Skyler still volunteered at the shelter.

"When I can."

"They need computer help over there."

"They need everything help."

"Ain't that the truth."

Skyler buckled Alexis into her seat.

The older man watched.

"Good-looking girls."

"Thank you."

"They'll keep you straight."

Skyler laughed.

"They're trying."

The man pointed toward the church building.

"Don't let work steal all of it. You blink and they're grown."

People said things like that constantly to parents.

Skyler usually smiled and nodded because the warning felt too generic to absorb.

You blink and they're grown.

Everyone knew.

Knowing did not slow time.

On the drive home, Luna asked, "What did he mean, building the future?"

Skyler glanced in the mirror.

"Who?"

"Church guy."

"Pastor?"

"Yeah."

"He meant sometimes people worry so much about later that they don't pay attention to now."

"Do you do that?"

Children had no respect for rhetorical questions.

Skyler considered lying.

"Sometimes."

"Why?"

"Because I want later to be good."

Luna thought.

"What if now is good?"

Skyler gripped the wheel.

There were sentences adults paid therapists to help them reach that children produced between church and a fast-food drive-through.

"Then I should probably notice it."

"Yeah."

Summer said, "Can we get fries?"

Philosophy ended.

They got fries.

Years later, when Skyler's life became a battle over interpretation, he would return to that morning because nothing about it needed decoding.

The pastor's sentence applied because it applied.

The stranger's warning about children growing up was common because life was common in important ways.

Luna's question had no handler behind it.

The peppermints were just peppermints.

Not every meaningful thing was a message.

Some meaning existed because human beings kept discovering the same truths independently.

That distinction would become one of the hardest things for Skyler to relearn.

## Insert Chapter — Dirt Under the Fingernails

The nursery began before customers arrived.

Morning light flattened rows of plants into orderly green geometry. Water hissed through hoses. Pallets scraped concrete. Trucks backed toward loading areas with electronic beeps that became invisible after enough hours.

Skyler liked being there early.

Plants did not have opinions yet.

People would arrive later asking whether the maple would survive in too much shade, whether the roses would attract bees, whether a shrub could be planted beside a septic line, whether something advertised as deer-resistant had personally signed a contract with local deer.

Before opening, the work was physical.

Move this.

Water that.

Unload.

Sweep.

Check inventory.

Find the tree somebody swore had been set aside even though nobody could identify who had set it aside, for whom, or why the tag now said **SOLD** in handwriting belonging to no employee willing to claim it.

"Skyler," his coworker called.

"What?"

"You know where the Japanese maples went?"

"Outside."

"Helpful."

"I try."

"The small ones."

"Row four yesterday."

"Not there."

Skyler followed him.

They found them in row six.

"See," Skyler said. "System worked."

"System was wrong."

"But we debugged it."

"They're trees."

"Everything is software if you're annoying enough."

His coworker looked at him.

"That's why nobody asks what you're thinking."

Skyler laughed.

He had been working around plants long enough to know the difference between a thing that looked dead and a thing that was dead.

Dormancy offended impatient people.

Brown branches.

No flowers.

Nothing visible happening.

A customer wanted certainty immediately.

"Is this alive?"

Snap a twig.

Green beneath bark.

Alive.

Skyler loved that little test.

Not because it never failed.

Because it made the invisible condition inspectable.

Later he would want every human problem to offer the same courtesy.

Is the relationship alive?

Snap here.

Is the career alive?

Check beneath the bark.

Is trust alive?

Cut it open and inspect for green.

People did not survive that method.

The nursery also taught him that conditions mattered more than intention.

A customer could love a plant to death.

Too much water.

Wrong soil.

Wrong light.

Planting too deep.

Care without understanding still had consequences.

Skyler remembered that years later when people insisted their motives should excuse their effects.

*I was trying to help.*

So were many people drowning roots.

One afternoon, heat settled over the yard with enough weight to slow conversation.

Skyler and an older employee named Gene loaded stone edging into a truck.

Gene worked slowly and never seemed behind.

Skyler worked fast and always seemed to have four unfinished tasks.

"You get paid by the heartbeat?" Gene asked.

"What?"

"You run everywhere."

"Lots to do."

"Always is."

They loaded another stack.

Gene said, "You know why young men stay tired?"

Skyler smiled.

"Because old men take breaks and give speeches?"

Gene laughed.

"Because you think finishing the list is when life starts."

The sentence irritated Skyler because church had recently delivered a version of the same accusation.

"Everybody's got advice lately."

"That's because you're visibly dumb."

Skyler laughed.

They finished loading.

At lunch Skyler checked school assignments, answered messages, opened GitHub, closed GitHub, checked his bank account, regretted checking his bank account, and played three moves in an online chess game.

Gene ate a sandwich.

No phone.

Skyler noticed.

"You don't have a smartphone?"

"Truck."

"Why?"

"Because if people can reach you all the time, they do."

Skyler stared.

"That's it?"

"That's enough."

The simplicity felt alien.

Skyler's phone contained work, school, family, banking, chess, code notifications, social media, dating, news, maps, photographs, voice recordings, faith podcasts, Chinese lessons, and eventually fear.

It was not a device.

It was the hallway every part of life used to enter the same room.

Years later, when that hallway began feeling compromised, Skyler would experience the loss as if someone had entered every room at once.

But on this afternoon the phone only buzzed because a professor had graded something.

Skyler checked.

B.

"I'll take it," he said.

Gene kept eating.

"School?"

"Yeah."

"What you studying?"

"Information technology. Software development."

"Gonna get rich?"

Skyler smiled.

"That's the plan."

Gene nodded toward the rows of plants.

"Everybody needs a plan. Just don't get mad when reality doesn't read it."

Skyler would remember that too.

Reality did not read plans.

Neither did children.

Neither did love.

Neither did criminals.

Neither did algorithms.

Chess was perhaps the only place where both players agreed on the rules before the first move.

That was why Skyler loved it more as life became less orderly.

## Insert Chapter — The Line

Factory work compressed time differently.

A nursery day changed with sun and weather.

A factory day changed when the line stopped.

Until then, repetition ruled.

Motion.

Noise.

Material.

Quality checks.

Motion.

Noise.

Material.

The human body learned rhythms it never consciously agreed to learn.

Skyler could feel when something was wrong before seeing it.

A change in vibration.

A gap where noise belonged.

A worker's posture shifting from routine to attention.

As team lead, he lived in those changes.

"Skyler!"

He turned.

Line three.

Somebody pointed toward a jam.

He walked quickly.

Not ran.

Safety rules.

"What happened?"

"Feeder's hanging."

"Stop confirmed?"

"Yeah."

They followed procedure.

Not heroic.

Heroics around machinery were how fingers became stories people told new employees.

Lock.

Check.

Clear.

Restart.

The machine returned to rhythm.

A coworker slapped Skyler's shoulder.

"Magic."

"Extremely boring magic."

"Best kind."

That sentence stayed with him longer than expected.

Boring systems prevented catastrophe precisely because they were boring.

Later, when Skyler built software, he chased impressive features more naturally than boring controls.

Authentication.

Audit logs.

Permissions.

Backups.

Rate limits.

Governance.

Nobody posted screenshots bragging about a well-designed recovery procedure.

But boring systems were where trust lived.

The factory also taught him hierarchy.

Not conspiracy.

Hierarchy.

A worker could know something a lead did not.

A lead could know something a supervisor did not.

A supervisor could know what management wanted without knowing why.

Management could act on information from somewhere else and never explain the source.

From the floor, all of this looked like one entity called *the company*.

Inside it, people disagreed constantly.

That experience would later become essential when Skyler tried to understand government and large organizations.

From outside, institutions looked like players.

Inside, they looked like boards crowded with pieces moving under overlapping rules.

One afternoon his supervisor called him into an office.

"Close the door."

Skyler did.

"Everything okay?"

"That's what I was going to ask you."

"Why?"

The supervisor leaned back.

"You're taking on a lot. School. Family. This."

"I'm fine."

"That's not what I asked."

Skyler smiled.

"People keep saying that to me."

"Maybe listen."

There was no accusation that day.

No rumor.

No security concern.

Just a supervisor noticing fatigue.

Skyler felt defensive anyway.

"My work bad?"

"No."

"Attendance?"

"No."

"Then what's the problem?"

The supervisor sighed.

"There isn't always a problem. Sometimes somebody asks if you're okay because they want to know if you're okay."

Skyler looked down.

He was already learning to hear questions as audits.

Not because someone trained him to.

Because life had begun making every domain feel evaluative.

Work evaluated performance.

School evaluated assignments.

Court evaluated credibility.

Relationships evaluated trust.

Social media evaluated worth through numbers.

Chess evaluated every move.

Skyler began living as if an invisible bar hovered beside him.

Winning.

Losing.

Good.

Bad.

Ahead.

Behind.

The habit predated the network.

That was why the network would later exploit it so efficiently.

You could not install a fear that had no port.

You could only discover which port was already open.

The supervisor said, "Take Friday if you need it."

Skyler shook his head.

"Need the hours."

"Figured."

He returned to the floor.

The line moved.

People worked.

Nothing mysterious happened.

That ordinary day mattered because later Skyler would remember the office and wonder if the supervisor had already been hearing rumors.

He would search the timeline.

Did somebody contact the company then?

Was that when it started?

Maybe.

Probably not.

The truthful answer was that concern could exist before conspiracy.

That did not make later interference impossible.

It made chronology harder.

Reality refused clean opening moves.

## Insert Chapter — Night School

The university existed mostly as tabs.

Course dashboard.

Discussion board.

Assignment rubric.

Grade center.

Email.

Library.

Skyler attended school in rooms that were physically somewhere else.

Kitchen table.

Break room.

Parking lot.

Bedroom after the girls slept.

A fast-food restaurant because the Wi-Fi at home had failed.

Once, the passenger seat of his car while rain hammered the windshield hard enough to make reading difficult.

Education fit around the life that paid for education.

He resented that and loved it.

At 11:42 p.m., a discussion-board prompt asked:

**How should organizations balance security with usability?**

Skyler stared at it.

The question seemed insultingly small compared with his real life.

Then he began typing.

> Security that prevents legitimate people from accessing a system can become its own form of failure. Usability without security creates different failure. The real goal is not maximizing one variable. It is designing boundaries appropriate to the consequences of error.

He stopped.

Read the last sentence again.

Boundaries appropriate to the consequences of error.

That was better than the assignment deserved.

He saved it to another file.

Skyler collected sentences the way some people collected tools.

The right sentence could unlock thinking.

That habit was another vulnerability later.

If a stranger repeated one of his sentences, it felt like access to the private workshop of his mind.

But writers reused language.

Students posted assignments.

Cloud software synced drafts.

Clipboard histories existed.

Browser extensions existed.

Autocorrect services existed.

People overheard.

People remembered.

A phrase could escape without a hacker.

Skyler knew that intellectually.

Years later, intellect would have to fight adrenaline for jurisdiction.

He submitted the assignment at 11:57.

Two minutes to spare.

The portal displayed:

**Submission successful.**

Tiny dopamine.

He opened GitHub.

A repository had a failing check.

Tiny dread.

He opened chess.

Opponent had moved.

Tiny challenge.

He checked a message from the girls' mother.

One child had a school event next week.

Tiny logistical crisis.

The phone compressed all scales of importance into rectangles of identical size.

A grade and a custody message arrived through the same vibration.

A spam ad and a bank warning used the same screen.

A stranger and a daughter both appeared as notifications.

Human nervous systems had not evolved for that equality.

Skyler responded to everything as if urgency were a property of arrival.

At 12:20, he opened a Chinese lesson.

The four tones became his favorite kind of frustration.

A sound could remain visually identical in Latin letters while becoming a different word when spoken differently.

Tone changed meaning.

Context changed meaning.

Sequence changed meaning.

Skyler practiced until the app accepted him.

He recorded himself.

Listened.

Cringed.

Tried again.

Then the oldest girl's voice came from the hallway.

"Dad?"

He pulled one earbud out.

"Yeah?"

"Why are you saying mom over and over?"

Skyler laughed.

"Chinese."

"That doesn't sound Chinese."

"Thank you for the support."

She entered the room, hair messy from sleep.

"Say my name."

"In Chinese?"

"Yeah."

"Your name is still your name."

"Then what's the point?"

"Excellent question."

She climbed into a chair.

Skyler showed her the tones.

She imitated them badly on purpose.

He corrected her.

She exaggerated more.

Within minutes both were laughing too loudly for midnight.

"Go to bed," he finally said.

"You go to bed."

"I'm an adult."

"That's not fair."

"Correct. Bed."

She left.

Skyler looked at the language app.

Meaning depended on tone.

The same sentence could become love, threat, joke, warning, manipulation, or nonsense depending on context.

Years later, that lesson would return in darker form.

An official-looking email could be legitimate or intimidation.

A song could be art or signal.

A photograph could be memory or threat.

A man in a suit could be consultant, fixer, criminal, former official, current contractor, liar, or several at once.

Tone mattered.

But tone was not proof.

He closed the app.

Opened the family message.

Added the school event to his calendar.

Then opened GitHub again because some lessons required repetition before they became wisdom.

## Insert Chapter — Hope Has Verbs

Skyler returned to Hope Campus on a cold morning carrying donated charging cables and believing this counted as a sophisticated technology intervention.

A volunteer looked into the box.

"These all work?"

"Probably."

She stared.

"That is not the word you use with donations."

"I tested most."

"Most?"

"Fine. I'll test all of them."

"Thank you."

The shelter had changed slightly.

New paint in one area.

Different posters.

Same coffee smell.

People at the same tables were not the same people, but the patterns of need remained recognizable.

Charging cable.

Bus route.

Job application.

Identification.

Housing list.

A place to sit where nobody expected a purchase.

Skyler spent an hour helping a man recover access to an email account.

The man had changed phone numbers.

The recovery system wanted the old number.

The backup email had been created years earlier and forgotten.

Security questions were answers from a life that no longer existed.

"It keeps telling me prove I'm me," the man said.

Skyler paused.

The sentence felt larger than the task.

"Yeah."

"I'm right here."

"I know."

"Computer don't."

They eventually found a recovery path.

The account opened.

Inside were messages about employment, benefits, family, and one old photograph the man stared at for a long time.

"Thought I lost this."

Skyler looked away to give privacy.

Identity systems were not abstract.

Lose access and you could lose continuity with parts of yourself.

That experience would later inform Aegis and Four Doors more honestly than any investor pitch.

At lunch, the chess player with the sugar-packet pawn was not there.

Skyler asked about him.

A volunteer shrugged.

"Haven't seen him in a while."

"Know where he went?"

"No."

Skyler felt an unexpected sadness.

People disappeared from shelters in both good and bad directions.

Housing.

Jail.

Hospital.

Family.

Another city.

Street.

Death.

Nobody always knew which.

Uncertainty existed long before Skyler considered it an enemy.

The difference was scale.

At the shelter, not knowing could remain not knowing.

Later, fear would turn unknown into a vacuum that demanded theories.

Skyler set up the chessboard anyway.

The black pawn was still missing.

He reached for a sugar packet.

A young man across the table laughed.

"That's ghetto chess."

"Innovation."

"That's sugar."

"Temporary pawn infrastructure."

"You one of those tech dudes?"

"Unfortunately."

They played.

The young man moved quickly, then faster when behind.

Skyler recognized himself.

"Slow down."

"Why?"

"Position's not running away."

"Clock is."

"It's fifteen minutes."

"Exactly."

Skyler smiled.

He won after the young man sacrificed a rook for an attack that looked dangerous and led nowhere.

"Again," the young man said.

"What'd you learn?"

"That you talk too much."

"Correct. Anything about chess?"

"Don't sacrifice stuff for no reason."

Skyler looked at the sugar-packet pawn.

"That's enough."

Before leaving, he helped clean tables.

No app.

No platform.

No AI.

No token.

Wet rag.

Trash bag.

Stacked chairs.

Hope had verbs.

Years later, when Skyler became tempted by the idea that his legacy needed to be enormous to matter, he would remember that morning.

A man recovered an email.

A stranger played chess.

A table became clean for the next person.

Small work did not become meaningless because it could not scale.

That insight would eventually save Four Doors Lab from becoming another giant thing Skyler wanted to own because owning it felt safer than trusting it.

## Insert Chapter — Before the Archive Became a Case

The first family archive lived in a folder called `kids`.

No encryption.

No elaborate naming convention.

No chain of custody.

Just photographs dumped from phones whenever storage became annoying.

`IMG_4482.JPG`

`VID_20210314_1922.mp4`

`Screenshot_2021-05-07.png`

Human history disguised as filenames machines invented.

Skyler occasionally tried to organize it.

`Luna`

`Summer`

`Alexis`

Then immediately encountered photographs containing all three.

`Family`

Then relationships changed and the word *family* became emotionally complicated.

`Kids Together`

Too clinical.

He gave up.

One night he opened random files.

Luna missing front teeth.

Summer asleep with a book upside down.

Alexis wearing a bowl as a hat.

A birthday cake leaning slightly left because Skyler had attempted decoration beyond his skill level.

A thirty-second video of everyone arguing about a balloon.

He laughed alone in the room.

Then cried so suddenly it irritated him.

"Come on."

He wiped his face.

The laptop did not care.

Digital memory had no mercy setting.

Photographs preserved moments without preserving the conditions needed to survive them.

A happy image did not show the argument twenty minutes earlier.

A sad image did not show laughter after.

A screenshot of an angry text did not contain the week around it.

Evidence froze fragments.

Narrative decided what the fragments meant.

Skyler had not learned that yet.

He made a second folder:

`IMPORTANT`

Inside he saved messages that made him feel like a good father.

Then, ashamed of himself, he created another:

`HARD_STUFF`

Messages where he had failed.

Missed call.

Angry reply.

Promise made too quickly.

An apology.

A schedule confusion that became an argument.

He did not know why he kept them.

Maybe because love without accountability felt like propaganda.

Maybe because part of him already imagined a future courtroom where a stranger asked what kind of father he had been and Skyler could submit an exhibit titled **ALL OF IT**.

No court accepted that exhibit.

Life did not either.

People were too large for folders.

The archive could support memory.

It could not become memory.

Years later, the Truth Protocol would formalize that distinction.

But before it had a name, Skyler sat alone scrolling through photographs and made a promise he would repeatedly fail and repeatedly return to:

Do not let documentation replace living.

Then he checked the time.

2:03 a.m.

He had school work due.

Of course.

He closed the photographs.

Opened the assignment.

The ordinary world continued asking things from him.
