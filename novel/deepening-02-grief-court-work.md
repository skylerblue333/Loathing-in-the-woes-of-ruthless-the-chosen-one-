# DEEPENING CHAPTERS II
## Grief, Court, Work, and the Cost of Being Described

---

## Insert Chapter — The Hallway

The courthouse hallway was quieter than Skyler expected.

Not solemn.

Not cinematic.

Quiet in the way offices became quiet when everybody was waiting for someone else's name.

Plastic chairs lined the wall. A vending machine hummed near a corner. A woman in scrubs whispered into a phone. A man wearing work boots stared at paperwork without turning a page.

Skyler sat with a folder on his lap.

Inside:

Messages.

Schedules.

Photographs.

Receipts.

Screenshots.

A calendar he believed proved something important until his lawyer asked what exactly he thought it proved.

"That I was there."

"Where?"

"Home. With the kids."

"On which date?"

Skyler searched.

The folder suddenly felt less like evidence and more like panic organized with paper clips.

His lawyer watched him.

"This is why we prepare."

"I know."

"No, you don't. You're still trying to bring your entire life into the room."

"Because they're talking about my entire life."

"The court isn't deciding your entire life today."

"Feels like it."

"Feelings and scope are different things."

Skyler hated the sentence.

Later he would repeat versions of it to himself voluntarily.

At the time it sounded like another professional telling him the part of reality he could feel most strongly did not count.

His former fiancée sat farther down the hallway with someone from her family.

They did not look at each other.

That avoidance hurt more than open hostility might have.

Once, they had known each other's sleeping faces.

Now they coordinated eye contact through absence.

A daughter sat beside Skyler's mother swinging her feet beneath a chair.

"Why are we here?"

His mother said, "Grown-up stuff."

"Is somebody in trouble?"

Skyler looked up.

Nobody answered fast enough.

Children noticed delays.

"No," he said.

His lawyer turned toward him but did not correct him.

The girl looked at the courtroom door.

"Is the judge a doctor?"

Skyler almost smiled.

"No."

"Then why does he decide if people are okay?"

The hallway disappeared for a second.

Not literally.

Emotionally.

The question reached something beneath legal strategy.

Adults had built systems where strangers decided what words meant, what risk meant, what credibility meant, what access meant, what family arrangement was safe enough.

Somebody had to decide.

That was what institutions were for.

But being the person described by an institution felt different from being the person who needed the institution to exist.

Skyler crouched.

"Different kind of job."

The child nodded.

Good enough.

Children accepted incomplete explanations when adults said them gently.

Adults rarely did.

The bailiff called a name that was not theirs.

Everyone waited again.

Skyler opened his folder.

His lawyer put a hand over it.

"Stop reviewing."

"I just want to make sure—"

"You will never make sure enough."

Skyler looked at him.

"What does that mean?"

"It means uncertainty isn't a preparation error. It's part of the process."

There it was again.

The thing Skyler would spend years fighting.

Not knowing.

He wanted a complete record because complete records promised control.

If he could reconstruct every conversation, every date, every sentence, then nobody could rewrite him.

But no archive could contain tone.

No screenshot preserved the five minutes before a message.

No receipt proved what kind of father a man had been on a Tuesday night when nobody was watching.

When they finally entered the courtroom, Skyler became intensely aware of posture.

His lawyer had coached him.

Do not react.

Do not interrupt.

Do not shake your head.

Do not whisper angrily.

Do not stare at the other side.

Answer what is asked.

Skyler sat with his hands clasped because even hands suddenly felt admissible.

A statement was made he believed was wrong.

His body moved before discipline.

A small shake of the head.

His lawyer touched his sleeve.

Skyler froze.

The lawyer did not look at him.

That touch contained the whole lesson.

Truth did not gain force merely because you reacted harder.

Sometimes reaction reduced your ability to communicate it.

The judge asked a question.

Skyler answered too much.

His lawyer redirected.

The other side answered too little.

Their lawyer clarified.

Everybody translated life into the grammar of a process.

At one point, Skyler heard himself described using an adjective he despised.

"Unstable."

The word was not a diagnosis.

Not a finding.

A characterization in an argument.

Still, it entered his body like a verdict.

He wanted to stand and list every stable thing he had done.

Work.

School.

Meals.

Appointments.

Bedtimes.

Bills.

Volunteer hours.

Code.

Laundry.

Driving.

All the ordinary repetitions of adulthood that disappeared inside one adjective.

He remained seated.

Especially then.

Afterward, in the hallway, his lawyer said, "You did okay."

Skyler laughed once.

"Okay?"

"Yes."

"What happens now?"

"We wait."

Of course.

The institution's favorite verb.

He walked outside into sunlight bright enough to feel disrespectful.

Cars moved.

Somebody carried groceries across a parking lot.

A lawn crew worked across the street.

Skyler felt offended that ordinary life had continued during the hearing.

He had experienced a tectonic event.

The world had experienced Thursday.

His phone buzzed.

A work message asking whether he could cover a shift.

He stared.

Then laughed harder.

The universe did not organize itself around narrative importance.

That was perhaps the first thing he should have learned from the day.

He learned it years later instead.

## Insert Chapter — The Apartment With No Toys

There is a particular kind of quiet after children leave a room.

Not silence.

Evidence of sound.

A cartoon paused.

A cup abandoned.

A blanket twisted.

A tiny plastic object exactly where an adult foot will eventually find it in darkness.

The first apartment Skyler lived in without the girls full-time had almost none of that.

He bought a small basket for toys.

It remained too organized.

That bothered him.

A child's room should look slightly occupied by weather.

He put stuffed animals on the bed.

Too staged.

He moved them.

Still staged.

He bought crayons.

Put them in a cup.

The cup stayed upright for six days.

Unacceptable.

He knocked it over himself one night, immediately felt ridiculous, and picked everything up.

Grief made adults perform strange rituals when nobody watched.

Skyler tried to create evidence of future normalcy.

Extra toothbrushes.

Snacks the girls liked.

A game.

Coloring books.

A cheap chess set.

The apartment became a waiting room for fatherhood.

On days the girls visited, it became alive too quickly.

On days they left, the transformation reversed before Skyler's nervous system caught up.

He found one pink sock after a visit and left it on the floor for three days.

Not because he forgot.

Because picking it up felt like acknowledging absence.

He eventually washed it and placed it in a drawer.

The drawer held exactly one sock.

That was somehow worse.

He worked more.

Studied more.

Coded more.

Played chess more.

People praised productivity because they could not see what was underneath it.

"You're killing it," someone commented beneath a project post.

Skyler stared at the sentence.

He had slept four hours and cried in the car that morning.

Online, he was killing it.

This was another early lesson in split identity.

Public Skyler and private Skyler were not false and true versions.

Both were true.

The mistake was assuming either one was complete.

He posted progress because progress existed.

He felt destroyed because destruction existed too.

A person could be succeeding and failing simultaneously across different boards.

Chess only had one board.

Life was unfair that way.

One evening, Luna called.

"What are you doing?"

Skyler looked at the laptop.

"Homework."

"Still?"

"Always."

"Why?"

"Because I made educational choices."

She laughed.

"Can you show me the apartment?"

Skyler switched to video.

He walked around.

"There's your bed."

"That's not my blanket."

"It can be your blanket."

"I want the unicorn one."

"Noted."

"Where's Summer's stuff?"

"Other drawer."

"And Alexis?"

"Other other drawer."

"That's confusing."

"My organizational system is sophisticated."

She demanded to see the refrigerator.

"Why?"

"I want to know what food you have."

Skyler opened it.

Eggs.

Milk.

Leftovers.

Energy drink.

Condiments.

"That's sad," Luna said.

"Excuse me?"

"You need snacks."

"I have adult snacks."

"Those aren't snacks."

"You have become judgmental."

She laughed again.

After the call, Skyler went to the store.

He bought fruit snacks, crackers, juice, cereal, and the unicorn blanket.

The purchase was financially stupid.

He did it anyway.

The apartment looked more like a place children might return to.

That night, he slept better.

Not because the custody issues were resolved.

Not because the relationship healed.

Because grief sometimes accepted props.

A blanket could become a promise.

That was not rational.

Neither was love.

Years later, when strangers used children's imagery to scare him, they were exploiting exactly this architecture.

A yellow sweatshirt was not merely yellow.

A blue cup was not merely plastic.

A seashell was not merely decoration.

Objects accumulated emotional permissions.

The network did not invent those permissions.

Family did.

That was why using them felt profane.

## Insert Chapter — The Rule of Three

After the breakup, Skyler made three rules for dating.

He broke all of them within a month.

Rule one: do not talk about the ex.

Rule two: do not date to make anybody jealous.

Rule three: do not confuse attention with connection.

Marcus heard the list and laughed for almost a full minute.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You're being an asshole."

"I'm being realistic. You made rules because you know exactly what you're about to do."

"I'm not."

"Okay."

Skyler hated Marcus's *okay* because it meant the argument had been archived for future use.

The first date was loud.

Music, television, people talking over each other.

Skyler liked that because silence created opportunities for seriousness.

The woman across from him asked what he did.

He gave the compressed version.

"Software. School. Kids."

"How many kids?"

"Three girls."

"Oh wow."

There were many ways people said *oh wow*.

Admiration.

Concern.

Calculation.

Skyler heard all of them whether present or not.

"Yeah."

"You divorced?"

"Never married. Engaged. It's complicated."

Rule one broke eleven minutes in.

By dessert, he had described enough relationship history to qualify as emotional discovery.

The woman listened kindly.

That kindness made him talk more.

When he got home, he felt relieved.

Then ashamed.

Then he checked whether his ex had viewed a social-media story from the evening.

Rule two broken.

He stared at the viewer list.

Why did it matter?

Because he wanted evidence that he mattered to somebody who had chosen life without him.

That was not romance.

It was an injured ego checking telemetry.

He closed the app.

Opened it five minutes later.

The second woman he dated worked nights.

She had the sharp humor of someone who had spent years dealing with men who thought money purchased personality.

"So you're a software guy?"

"Yeah."

"Can you fix my phone?"

"Probably not."

"Good answer."

"Most software people are terrible at phones."

"Then what do you do?"

"Argue with computers in more expensive ways."

She laughed.

They talked until two in the morning.

Skyler felt seen.

That was dangerous because grief translated being seen into being rescued.

Within days, he checked messages too often.

A delayed reply changed his mood.

A warm reply restored it.

Rule three broke quietly.

Attention became medication.

The woman noticed before he did.

"You need to chill."

Skyler stared at the message.

Three words.

No punctuation.

He immediately began interpreting tone.

Angry?

Playful?

Annoyed?

Ending things?

He typed:

**What do you mean?**

Deleted.

Typed:

**lol fair**

Deleted.

Typed:

**Sorry**

Deleted.

His Chinese lessons should have warned him.

Tone changed meaning.

Text erased tone.

Human beings filled the gap with whatever fear had nearest.

He finally sent:

**😂 probably true**

She replied with a heart.

Crisis over.

No crisis had existed.

That small scene mattered because later anonymous messages would exploit the same machinery.

The network did not need to create a new cognitive weakness.

It only needed to send text into a nervous system already trained to infer tone from silence.

One night the woman asked about the girls.

Skyler showed photographs.

Too many.

Parents always showed too many.

"They're cute."

"Yeah."

"You miss them."

Not a question.

Skyler looked down.

"Yeah."

She reached across the table and touched his hand.

No advice.

No investigation.

No promise.

Just contact.

Years later, he would remember that moment when people tried to turn every relationship into a faction.

Not everybody entered his life because someone sent them.

Some people entered because human beings met each other.

Some left because timing failed.

Some hurt each other without handlers.

Some kindness had no hidden motive.

Suspicion became survivable only after Skyler learned to preserve a category for ordinary human contact.

## Insert Chapter — Seven Interviews, One Shirt

Skyler owned one interview shirt he trusted.

Blue.

Not bright.

Not dark enough to look like he was trying too hard.

He wore it until the collar softened.

For video interviews he paired it with whatever pants happened to exist because civilization had agreed webcams ended at the waist.

The first interview went well.

The second went better.

The third interviewer asked him to explain a project he had described too grandly online.

Skyler stumbled.

"It's more of a prototype."

"Your profile says platform."

"Yeah. I need to update that."

The interviewer did not attack him.

Did not accuse him of fraud.

Just wrote something down.

Skyler felt exposed anyway.

Afterward, he rewrote the project README.

**Prototype** instead of **production platform**.

**Explores** instead of **provides**.

**Planned** instead of **supports**.

The edits felt like shrinking.

Then they felt like relief.

Make the claim match the code.

That rule came partly from embarrassment.

Useful rules often did.

Another interview ended because Skyler talked too much.

He knew this because the recruiter told him kindly.

"Your answers are thoughtful, but you can be more concise."

Skyler almost replied with a four-paragraph explanation of why.

He caught himself.

"That's fair."

Growth.

A third employer rejected him after a background process.

No explanation.

That one hurt differently.

He asked whether there was inaccurate information.

The company sent standardized language about consumer-report rights.

Skyler requested what he could.

The report contained nothing dramatic.

No secret blacklist.

No conspiracy note.

Some ordinary items.

He felt relieved and disappointed simultaneously.

An enemy would have explained more.

A fourth employer hired him and later reduced hours.

A fifth contract disappeared after a client budget change.

A sixth ended with conflict partly caused by Skyler's own frustration.

A seventh contained the strange security review.

When he later told the story as "seven jobs," memory lined them up like coordinated attacks.

But the actual sequence resisted symmetry.

Some failures were his.

Some were theirs.

Some were economic.

Some were mysterious.

One or two later acquired evidence of outside pressure.

The mixture became important.

If every loss had been engineered, Skyler would have been innocent in a way real people rarely are.

If none had been influenced, the later evidence would have meant nothing.

Reality gave him the harder story:

He had genuine weaknesses **and** people exploited them.

He had made mistakes **and** been manipulated.

He was not required to choose which category owned him.

One afternoon, before another interview, Skyler noticed a stain near the cuff of the blue shirt.

He rubbed it with water.

Worse.

He laughed.

"Perfect."

He wore it anyway.

The interviewer did not mention the stain.

Of course not.

People noticed less than fear imagined.

Skyler got the job.

For three months, nothing strange happened.

That period rarely appeared in his later stories because normalcy had poor narrative retention.

He worked.

Got paid.

Made mistakes.

Fixed them.

A coworker disliked him.

Another became a friend.

The system did not target him.

The world did not rhyme.

Then one morning a manager used a phrase Skyler recognized from an anonymous message.

The old machinery returned instantly.

That contrast mattered.

Paranoia was not a permanent fog.

It was a switch that could remain off for weeks and activate in one sentence.

The network, when it later learned this, did not need constant pressure.

Intermittent pressure was more effective.

A slot machine paid occasionally to keep the player engaged.

A threat system needed only enough strange events to keep certainty unavailable.

## Insert Chapter — The First Time He Said Too Much

The social-media post was written at 3:08 a.m.

Nothing intelligent should be posted at 3:08 a.m.

Skyler knew this at 10:00 a.m.

At 3:08, it felt necessary.

He had lost sleep after another employment setback and an argument with someone close to him. He had spent hours reviewing old screenshots. The screenshots had not changed. His interpretation had.

That was the first warning.

He wrote:

**Something is going on behind the scenes. Too many things line up. Jobs, accounts, people repeating stuff. I don't know who is involved but I'm done pretending it's all coincidence.**

Reasonable enough at first.

Then he kept writing.

Paragraphs.

Names omitted but categories implied.

Officials.

Hackers.

People at work.

People around relationships.

Maybe organized crime.

Maybe something bigger.

He used the phrase **government gangsters** publicly for the first time.

Not as a proven claim.

As anger.

The distinction disappeared once screenshotted.

By morning, the post had comments.

Some supportive.

Some mocking.

Some feeding the theory.

One stranger wrote:

**Bro you have no idea how deep this goes.**

Skyler clicked the profile.

Conspiracy content everywhere.

Another wrote:

**Get sleep. Seriously.**

Skyler interpreted that as dismissal.

A friend texted privately:

**Delete this before employers see it.**

Skyler interpreted that as concern mixed with shame.

His mother called.

"What are you doing?"

"Telling the truth."

"What truth?"

"That something's happening."

"Do you know what?"

"Not completely."

"Then why are you posting completely?"

He became angry.

"You don't believe me."

"I believe you're scared."

"That's not the same thing."

"No. It isn't."

The sentence felt cruel.

Years later, Skyler would understand it as one of the most respectful things she had said.

Believing fear was not the same as confirming attribution.

At 10:17, he deleted the post.

Too late.

Screenshots survived.

One would later appear in a risk-assessment file.

Another in an anonymous account mocking him.

Another in a thread defending him.

One act became evidence for incompatible stories.

Skyler had supplied the raw material himself.

That realization initially produced shame.

Then discipline.

If adversaries could weaponize public material, public restraint was not cowardice.

It was attack-surface reduction.

He hated that phrase because it turned personality into cybersecurity.

It was still useful.

At noon, Skyler wrote in a private note:

**Rule: don't publish at 3 a.m.**

Then:

**Rule: being upset is not a publication deadline.**

Then:

**Rule: if the claim is bigger than the evidence, shrink the claim.**

He would violate all three again.

But rules did not become worthless because humans failed to follow them perfectly.

They became paths back.

## Insert Chapter — What the Kids Remember

Adults remember crises by chronology.

Children remember them by texture.

Years later, when Skyler carefully asked Luna what she remembered from the worst period of family conflict, he expected courtrooms, arguments, houses, adults crying.

She said, "That apartment had weird carpet."

Skyler stared.

"What?"

"The brown carpet. It scratched."

"That's what you remember?"

"And you burned pancakes."

"I did not burn them."

"They were black."

"Caramelized."

She laughed.

Skyler felt something painful release.

His children had not experienced his story the way he narrated it.

Of course they had not.

They had their own.

Summer remembered a stuffed animal left in a car.

Alexis remembered almost nothing clearly from the earliest years except fragments other people had told her enough times that memory and story had mixed.

Skyler understood the danger immediately.

Adults could accidentally install memories by repeating narratives around children.

He became more careful.

Not perfect.

More careful.

"You know there was a lot going on," he told Luna once.

"Yeah."

"I don't want you to feel like you have to pick a side."

She looked confused.

"I'm not."

The simplicity embarrassed him.

Adults spent years constructing sides children sometimes refused to join.

"Good."

"Can we play chess?"

"Yes."

She beat him because he blundered a rook while thinking about emotional development.

"You cheated."

"No I didn't."

"I was distracted by responsible parenting."

"That's not a rule."

"Should be."

She laughed.

The game mattered more than the conversation because it returned them to something shared.

Skyler began understanding that legacy was not the final argument his children would read someday.

Legacy was accumulated contact.

Calls answered.

Jokes repeated.

Food remembered.

Apologies made.

Showing up.

Missing and trying again.

A father could leave documents explaining himself.

He could not litigate his way into a child's heart.

That truth would later shape the ending of the dossier.

His daughters would not become footnotes proving his goodness.

If the world wanted to know what kind of father he had been, the world would have to tolerate uncertainty.

His children were not public evidence.
