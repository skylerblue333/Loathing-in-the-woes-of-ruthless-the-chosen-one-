# DEEPENING CHAPTERS III
## The Digital Haunting

These chapters sit between the ordinary-life collapse and the first hard evidence. They deepen the difference between algorithmic creepiness, human harassment, technical compromise, and the mind's understandable tendency to combine them.

---

## Insert Chapter — The Phone Has Too Many Ears

Skyler knew phones listened.

That sentence was both true and misleading enough to ruin a week.

Microphones listened for wake words.

Applications requested microphone access.

Keyboards learned patterns.

Browsers synchronized histories.

Cloud services stored drafts.

Advertising systems profiled behavior.

Apps shared identifiers.

Contacts uploaded themselves into other people's services because someone else clicked **Allow** three years ago.

A modern phone could know too much without one person secretly sitting behind it wearing headphones.

Skyler understood that.

Then the phone did something creepy.

Understanding collapsed.

He had been talking aloud in the kitchen about needing a new mattress.

Not searching.

Talking.

Twenty minutes later, mattress advertisements appeared.

"See?" he said.

His friend Marcus looked at the screen.

"You've been complaining about your back for two weeks."

"I didn't search mattresses."

"You searched back pain."

"That's different."

"To us. Maybe not to advertising demons."

Skyler laughed.

They tested it badly.

For the next hour they repeatedly said unusual products near the phone.

"Alpaca socks."

"Industrial mayonnaise."

"Left-handed aquarium."

"That's not a thing."

"Exactly."

"Victorian shovel."

"Why Victorian?"

"Specificity."

No ads appeared.

Marcus declared victory.

Skyler declared the experiment scientifically useless.

Both were correct.

The incident mattered because it introduced a category error that would haunt him later.

A technology could behave invasively without a personal adversary.

A personal adversary could exploit invasive technology without controlling it.

And an ordinary person could experience both as one presence because both arrived through the same rectangle.

The phone became a stage where unrelated actors shared lighting.

Family.

School.

Work.

Advertisers.

Scammers.

Friends.

Strangers.

Algorithms.

Later, Ghost.

Later, Thorne.

Same screen.

Same vibration.

Different causality.

That was the first real horror of the device.

Not that it was possessed.

That too many systems could speak through it while looking equally immediate.

## Insert Chapter — The Screenshot That Changed

The timestamp changed.

Skyler was sure.

He had taken a screenshot at 2:22.

When he looked later, the message displayed 2:19.

Three minutes.

Small enough to sound ridiculous.

Large enough to disturb someone already tracking sequence.

He opened the original screenshot.

2:19.

"No."

He checked another.

2:19.

His handwritten note said 2:22.

Which was wrong?

He remembered looking at the clock.

Maybe the clock had shown 2:22 after he spent several minutes reading.

Maybe the app displayed message-send time while he recorded screenshot time.

Maybe one device had drift.

Maybe time zone handling.

Maybe cached display.

Maybe his memory.

Or manipulation.

That last possibility arrived with more emotional force than the others because it contained agency.

Somebody changed it.

Skyler began comparing clocks.

Phone.

Laptop.

Microwave.

Car.

Network time.

His apartment became a tiny observatory dedicated to three missing minutes.

The microwave was four minutes fast.

Car two minutes slow.

Laptop matched network time.

Phone matched within seconds.

No revelation.

At 4:00 a.m., Skyler realized he had spent nearly two hours attempting to explain a timestamp difference that could have been caused by how he recorded the event.

He wrote:

**I may be manufacturing precision after the fact.**

The sentence hurt.

He saved it anyway.

Months later, a genuinely altered timestamp would appear in an account export because a platform converted between local and UTC displays inconsistently.

Another apparently altered timestamp would turn out to be a screenshot from a different notification thread.

One would remain unexplained.

The lesson was not that timestamps lied.

The lesson was that clocks were evidence only after understanding what clock each system actually stored.

That distinction became one of Dev's favorite ways to annoy him.

"What time is it?" Skyler would ask.

"According to which source?"

"I hate you."

"Source provenance."

Truth became less dramatic and more technical.

That was progress.

## Insert Chapter — Recommendation Engine

Skyler built a toy recommendation model because he wanted to frighten himself scientifically.

He used public text from his own posts, project descriptions, interests, and liked topics.

No private messages.

No hacked data.

No microphone.

He fed the material into a simple categorization script and asked what content might keep a person like him engaged.

The outputs were insulting.

Chess.

Programming.

Cryptocurrency.

Cybersecurity.

Fatherhood.

Custody conflict.

Motivation.

Betrayal.

Mental resilience.

China.

Language learning.

Faith.

Government distrust.

Technology billionaires.

Conspiracy-adjacent content.

Career advice.

Romantic relationships.

"Well," he said.

The model had reconstructed a distressingly effective feed using information Skyler had published voluntarily.

Then he weighted recent engagement.

If he clicked hacked-account stories, cyber content increased.

If he watched custody videos, family-conflict content increased.

If he angrily commented on a political post, political conflict increased.

The feed became darker without any malicious operator.

Skyler sat back.

This did not disprove targeted harassment.

It explained how targeted harassment could hide inside ordinary recommendation behavior.

A malicious person would not need to control the algorithm.

They could feed it.

Tag him.

Send content.

Create engagement.

Make a topic salient.

Then let the platform continue.

Mara later called this **riding the current**.

The river already moved.

Influence operators only needed to toss the right object upstream.

Skyler hated the metaphor because it reduced the supernatural quality of what he had felt.

It also made the mechanism more plausible.

The feed was not a puppet.

It was weather someone could sometimes seed.

That was worse in a quieter way.

## Insert Chapter — The Help Desk

The most terrifying cybersecurity conversation Skyler ever had began with a bored technician saying, "Looks normal."

Skyler had brought the device to a repair shop after a series of battery drains, strange login prompts, and one microphone-permission indicator he could not explain.

He expected either vindication or dismissal.

The technician gave him troubleshooting.

Battery health degraded.

Several applications running background tasks.

Cloud synchronization active.

One browser extension with broad permissions.

No obvious malware in the checks available at the shop.

"So nobody hacked it?"

The technician looked at him.

"I can't say that."

"You just said it looks normal."

"I said what I'm seeing looks normal. That's not the same as proving nothing happened."

Skyler almost laughed.

Everybody had joined the cult of precise language.

"Can you tell if someone accessed my microphone?"

"Not historically in the way you're asking."

"Can you tell if an account was compromised?"

"Account provider logs might. Device won't answer all of that."

"So what can you tell me?"

The technician pointed at the charging port.

"Your cable is trash."

Skyler stared.

"That's your finding?"

"One finding."

The cable connection had been causing intermittent charge behavior Skyler had interpreted as unexplained device activity.

He felt stupid.

Then angry at himself for feeling stupid.

Finding an ordinary explanation for one symptom did not erase every unexplained event.

But fear preferred binary verdicts.

Hacked or not hacked.

Targeted or not targeted.

Safe or unsafe.

Real systems produced partial answers.

The technician handed the phone back.

"Change passwords from a trusted device. Use multifactor. Remove extensions you don't need. Update everything. Check account sessions. If you have specific evidence, preserve it."

"And if I think someone sophisticated is after me?"

The technician shrugged.

"Then don't make me pretend this counter is a forensics lab."

Skyler respected him immediately.

Expertise included knowing the boundary of the tool.

Years later, that sentence would influence how Four Doors Lab presented its own capabilities.

No magical dashboards.

No **SECURE** badge pretending risk disappeared.

State what can be known.

State what cannot.

The technician sold him a new cable.

Skyler paid too much for it.

When several weird charging symptoms vanished, he entered them into Truth Protocol retroactively:

**Resolved ordinary cause.**

He did not delete the old fear.

He annotated it.

That was a more mature form of memory.

## Insert Chapter — The Account That Knew Too Much

The anonymous profile had no followers and followed one account: Skyler's.

That detail felt intimate.

He clicked through.

Created two days ago.

No posts.

No biography.

No image.

The account sent one line:

**You still leave the knight by the laptop?**

Skyler's body went cold.

The black knight sat beside the laptop.

He looked at it.

Then at the window.

Curtains closed.

His first thought was camera compromise.

Second: somebody had been inside.

Third: his children had mentioned it.

Fourth: he had photographed the desk before.

He searched his own public posts.

There.

Six months earlier.

A GitHub progress photo.

Laptop screen, coffee cup, code.

Black knight visible near the edge.

Caption:

**late-night debugging, knight keeping watch 😂**

Skyler sat back.

The message was creepy because the sender selected a forgotten public detail and presented it as current private knowledge.

Technique.

Not omniscience.

He replied:

**Nice try. Public photo.**

The account responded:

**Good.**

Then:

**Now find the things you didn't post.**

The profile disappeared.

Skyler stared.

That was worse than if the trick had remained unexplained.

Someone was teaching him to distrust the boundary between public and private.

Why?

To frighten him?

Train him?

Recruit him?

Test him?

Or merely enjoy the reaction?

The message could have come from Ghost before she introduced herself.

Mara later denied it.

Skyler never proved otherwise.

Unknown remained unknown.

Not every mystery received a satisfying owner.

That itself became a form of discipline.

## Insert Chapter — Blue Sedan Experiment

Skyler conducted another terrible experiment.

After dark-blue sedans began appearing everywhere in his attention, he decided to count them.

For one week, every time he drove more than ten minutes, he recorded how many dark-blue sedans he noticed.

Day one: seven.

Day two: eleven.

Day three: four.

Day four: thirteen.

The number seemed absurdly high.

Then Lena asked, "How many silver SUVs?"

Skyler stared.

"I didn't count."

"How many white trucks?"

"Didn't count."

"Then your denominator is vibes."

"My denominator is not vibes."

"Very scientific response."

He repeated the experiment counting four categories.

White trucks dominated.

Silver SUVs nearly matched blue sedans.

The blue vehicles stopped looking impossible.

Selective attention explained part of the experience.

Then, on the sixth day, a dark-blue sedan with a specific partial plate appeared near his workplace and later near his apartment.

Now the category contained a different event.

Same color.

Different evidence.

Skyler photographed the plate from a distance.

He did not approach.

The car belonged to a rental fleet.

That fact explained nothing and several things.

Could be coincidence.

Could be contractor.

Could be different drivers.

Later, an invoice would connect a rental from that fleet to a security subcontractor during the same week.

Not the exact plate conclusively.

A probability increase, not a movie reveal.

Skyler learned to stop asking whether **blue sedans** were evidence.

Some blue sedans were cars.

One might have been surveillance.

Categories did not inherit evidence from their most suspicious member.

That lesson generalized painfully.

Some officials were corrupt.

That did not make officials corrupt as a category.

Some contractors abused access.

That did not make every contractor part of the operation.

Some songs were commissioned.

That did not make music a messaging system.

Some people lied.

That did not make trust impossible.

The mind liked nouns.

Evidence liked instances.

## Insert Chapter — Algorithmic Gaslighting

Mara disliked the phrase *algorithmic gaslighting*.

"Algorithms don't have intent," she said.

Skyler replied, "The experience can still feel like it."

"Then say that."

"Nobody titles a chapter 'The Experience Resembled Gaslighting Though Intent Was Distributed.'"

"You might."

"Fair."

They were arguing over terminology because terminology was safer than arguing over guilt.

Mara explained how recommendation systems could amplify a target's existing fear without anyone manually selecting each item.

Suppose an operator wanted Skyler thinking about surveillance.

They did not need platform control.

They might send a few posts through accounts likely to reach him.

He watches.

He searches.

He comments.

The platform observes engagement and supplies more surveillance material.

Then the operator screenshots the resulting feed and says:

**See? Everybody is talking about you.**

The platform becomes an unpaid accomplice without knowing any target exists.

"That's disgusting," Skyler said.

"Yes."

"You did this?"

Mara paused.

"I designed audience-seeding experiments."

"On me?"

"Not originally."

"Eventually?"

"Yes."

Skyler looked away.

The room became too small.

Mara continued because confession did not become kinder by stopping before the ugly nouns.

"Some content was generic. We measured whether you'd interact. Some was adapted after public posts. One contractor bought engagement from low-quality accounts. The system did the rest."

"Songs?"

"A few."

"Which?"

"I don't know every one."

"Convenience store?"

"No evidence we controlled that playlist."

"Passing car?"

"Could have been a contractor. Could have been random."

"Church?"

"Not us. As far as I know."

"Jobs?"

"One reputation inquiry. Maybe two. Not seven."

Skyler felt his story shrinking and hardening simultaneously.

Less total.

More defensible.

He hated Mara for the reduction because some part of him had become emotionally dependent on the idea that scale validated pain.

If only two jobs were influenced, did the others hurt less?

No.

If only three songs were commissioned, did the other moments stop being frightening when he experienced them?

No.

The mistake was thinking external authorship determined emotional legitimacy.

A coincidence could terrify.

A deliberate act could fail to.

Feeling and causality were different axes.

Mara said, "You know the worst part?"

"There are rankings?"

"Once you started publicly connecting everything, some people in the network stopped doing anything and took credit anyway."

Skyler looked at her.

"Thorne."

"Especially Thorne."

"Why?"

"Because free fear has excellent margins."

That sentence became one of the darkest in the book.

Not because it described advanced technology.

Because it described ordinary opportunism.

## Insert Chapter — The Night the Feed Went Quiet

For twenty-four hours, Skyler did not use social media.

He expected peace.

Instead he experienced withdrawal.

Not chemical in the strict sense.

Behavioral.

He reached for the phone during every gap.

Bathroom.

Red light.

Microwave.

Commercial break.

Elevator.

Waiting for coffee.

The feed had become a reflexive answer to micro-boredom.

Without it, his own thoughts became louder.

That was uncomfortable.

By evening, he noticed something else.

The world generated fewer coincidences when he consumed fewer inputs.

Obvious in retrospect.

Fewer posts meant fewer opportunities for a phrase to match his day.

Fewer songs meant fewer lyrics to feel directed.

Fewer ads meant fewer uncanny product suggestions.

Reduced exposure did not prove the feed had been innocent.

It changed the base rate.

Skyler wrote:

**If you inspect one thousand random sentences, some will feel personal. If you inspect ten, fewer chances.**

Then:

**But deliberate operators can exploit high-volume environments because coincidence provides camouflage.**

Both.

Always both.

The next morning, after twenty-four quiet hours, an SMS arrived from an unknown number:

**You can't mute people who know where you live.**

Skyler's stomach dropped.

The message hit harder because the feed had gone quiet.

One direct intrusion among low noise.

He preserved it.

Did not answer.

Called the appropriate contact.

Documented.

No public post.

The sender had attempted to convert quiet into panic.

Skyler refused to supply the performance.

That was one of the first times he felt strategy becoming internal rather than reactive.

## Insert Chapter — Threat Model

Skyler's first threat model was embarrassing.

He listed everyone.

Former partner.

Unknown hackers.

Government.

Criminal groups.

Employers.

Strangers.

Private investigators.

Political actors.

Foreign actors.

Thorne.

Ghost.

"This isn't a threat model," Dev said.

"What is it?"

"An anxiety zoo."

Skyler laughed despite himself.

Dev rebuilt it around capabilities rather than dramatic identities.

**Public information access.**

**Purchased commercial data.**

**Credential compromise.**

**Insider administrative access.**

**Physical observation.**

**Social engineering.**

**Reputation influence.**

**Synthetic media.**

**Legal process.**

**Ordinary coincidence.**

"Coincidence isn't a threat actor," Skyler said.

"No, but your interpretation of coincidence is an attack surface."

Skyler stared.

"That's rude."

"Useful rude."

The model transformed fear.

If an attacker only had public data, change public exposure.

If credential compromise, secure accounts.

If insider access, logs and oversight mattered.

If physical observation, safety procedures.

If reputation influence, documentation and communication.

Different problems.

Different controls.

One omnipotent enemy required omnipotent defense.

Specific capabilities required specific response.

That was the technical version of the whole book.

Stop fighting the monster.

Name the move.
